Theory Fundamentals · Part 4

Key Signatures & the Cycle of Fourths

Notation · Major keys · Interactive exploration

In the previous lesson, we wrote sharps and flats in front of individual notes. When a piece uses the same sharps or flats again and again, there is a simpler way: write them once, at the beginning of the staff.

1. A simpler way to write music

Look at these two treble staves. They show exactly the same G major scale. On the left, F♯ is written as an accidental beside the note. On the right, the F♯ is written just after the clef, before the notes begin. That is a key signature.

Accidental beside the note
Key signature at the beginning

Both examples sound the same. The key signature makes the notation less cluttered, especially when the music is longer.

A key signature applies throughout the music until the signature changes. For example, one sharp in a key signature means every written F is normally played as F♯, regardless of its octave. A local accidental can temporarily change this, and a natural sign (♮) can cancel the sharp or flat for that note.

Quick check 1

A key signature shows one sharp: F♯. What happens when you see an F on the staff, without another accidental?

2. Key signatures build a pattern

Starting with C major, which has no sharps or flats, we can add them one at a time. The order is not random.

Sharps always accumulate in this order: F♯ – C♯ – G♯ – D♯ – A♯ – E♯ – B♯. Each new sharp moves the major tonic up a perfect fifth (or down a fourth).

Flats accumulate in the reverse letter order: B♭ – E♭ – A♭ – D♭ – G♭ – C♭ – F♭. Each new flat moves the major tonic up a perfect fourth.

3. The Cycle of Fourths

Now for the interesting pattern. Move from C to F, then B♭, then E♭. Each tonic is a perfect fourth above the previous one. With each move we add one flat, until we reach the enharmonic meeting point. At the six-accidental point, G♭ major and F♯ major sound the same but are spelled differently. We can then continue around the circle using sharp key signatures instead.

Moving in the other direction gives the cycle of fifths: C, G, D, A, E, B and so on. It is the same circle, travelled in reverse. These relationships will become important when we study chord progressions and jazz harmony.

One helpful shortcut: for sharp major keys, the last sharp in the signature is a semitone below the tonic. For flat major keys with two or more flats, the second-to-last flat names the major key. F major (one flat) is the exception to that second rule.

4. Learn to recognise key signatures

Look at the symbols written immediately after the treble clef. Count the sharps or flats and identify the corresponding major key. You can use the cycle above to help at first, then try without it.
