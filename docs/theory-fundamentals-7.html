Theory Fundamentals · Part 7

Seventh Chords

Stacking thirds · Four-note chords · Listening · Treble clef

We have already learned to build triads by stacking thirds: 1–3–5. What happens if we add one more third above the fifth? We get a four-note chord, often called a seventh chord.

1. From three notes to four

Start with a C major triad: C–E–G. Add B above G. The result, C–E–G–B, is C major seventh (Cmaj7). The new note is a seventh above the root because it is the seventh letter of the C scale when C is counted as 1.

Three notes: C major

C – E – G (1 – 3 – 5)

Four notes: C major seventh

C – E – G – B (1 – 3 – 5 – 7)

The seventh is measured from the root. It does not mean seven notes are played. It means that the new note is a seventh above the root, giving us four notes stacked in thirds.

Every degree, every seventh chord

A discovery: in major scales, the seventh chord on degree V is a dominant seventh. In harmonic minor, raising the seventh scale degree changes several chord qualities, including a major-minor seventh chord on V and a fully diminished seventh chord on vii. The chord's quality comes from the intervals between its actual notes.
