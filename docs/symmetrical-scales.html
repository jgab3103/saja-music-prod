Understanding Symmetrical Scales

1. What makes a scale symmetrical?

In the previous lessons we explored the major, harmonic minor and melodic minor collections. Each contains seven pitches, and each can be organised around different tonal centres. Here we explore collections constructed from repeating interval patterns.

In twelve-tone equal temperament, the octave contains twelve pitch classes. A pitch class includes all octave equivalents of a note: every C belongs to the same pitch class. A collection is transpositionally symmetrical when shifting every pitch by a particular number of semitones smaller than an octave produces the same collection of pitch classes.

C major

C–D–E–F–G–A–B

2–2–1–2–2–2–1 semitones. No smaller transposition reproduces the same set.

C whole-tone

C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯

2–2–2–2–2–2 semitones. Transposing by two semitones reproduces the same notes.

Same collection, different starting note: A scale may begin on any of its notes without changing its pitch-class content. Transposition is different: every note moves by the same interval. Symmetry occurs when this operation returns the original collection.

Symmetry can weaken the sense that one note must be home. Nevertheless, a bass note, repeated motif, accompaniment or resolution can establish a tonal centre even within a highly symmetrical collection.

2. The principal symmetrical scales

Whole-tone

C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯–C

Six notes; every interval is a whole tone. There are only two distinct collections. With no perfect fifth or semitone, the scale has a distinctive floating sound. It contains two augmented triads.

Half–whole diminished

C–D♭–E♭–E–F♯–G–A–B♭–C

Eight notes; alternates semitones and whole tones. Particularly useful over dominant seventh chords with ♭9, ♯9, ♯11 and 13.

Whole–half diminished

C–D–E♭–F–G♭–A♭–A–B–C

Eight notes; alternates whole tones and semitones. Contains the diminished seventh chord on C (C–E♭–G♭–B♭♭, with B♭♭ sounding as A).

Augmented

C–E♭–E–G–G♯–B–C

Six notes; alternates minor thirds and semitones. Contains two augmented triads, C–E–G♯ and E♭–G–B, and can colour major seventh or augmented harmony.

Chromatic

C–C♯–D–E♭–E–F–F♯–G–A♭–A–B♭–B–C

All twelve pitches. Every semitone transposition reproduces the collection. In improvisation, chromatic notes often connect or surround more stable notes.

Note spellings are chosen for readability and chord function. Enharmonically equivalent pitches are treated as the same pitch class in this lesson.

3. A brief historical and musical context

Late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

Composers increasingly explored pitch collections beyond ordinary major and minor tonality. Whole-tone and octatonic materials offered alternatives to conventional tonal direction. Listen to Debussy’s Voiles (from the first book of Préludes) for its extensive whole-tone writing, contrasted with a pentatonic passage.

Octatonic writing

The octatonic collection appears in the music of Rimsky-Korsakov and Stravinsky. Listen to the interaction between repeated patterns, contrasting harmonies and collections in Stravinsky’s Petrushka.

Messiaen and limited transposition

Olivier Messiaen systematised seven modes of limited transposition in his theoretical writings. The whole-tone and octatonic collections are his first two modes. We will explore the remaining modes and their compositional possibilities in a separate lesson.

Jazz and contemporary improvisation

Jazz musicians use symmetrical materials to explore altered dominant colours, diminished harmony and recurring melodic shapes. Symmetrical harmonic organisation also appears in John Coltrane’s Giant Steps, whose major-third key-centre cycle is a different phenomenon from playing the augmented scale itself.

4. Limited transposition and the symmetry lab

Ordinarily a scale can be transposed to twelve different pitch-class collections. A symmetrical collection repeats sooner. The smallest positive transposition that reproduces the set is also the number of distinct transpositions available.

Half–whole and whole–half are two orientations of the same octatonic family, not two separate families of three collections. C half–whole has exactly the same notes as D♭ whole–half.

These are practical interval labels, not strict diatonic spellings. For example, the sixth of the whole–half diminished scale is enharmonically the diminished seventh of its tonic diminished chord. The augmented scale’s minor third can also be heard as a sharp ninth against major harmony.

Listen before labelling: The same pitch can be heard differently depending on the chord beneath it. A tritone might be a flattened fifth or a raised eleventh. Its musical function is not determined by its spelling alone.

6. Symmetry and shared harmony

One of the most interesting consequences of symmetry is that a single collection can support several chords of the same quality. Consider C half–whole diminished:

C–D♭–E♭–E–F♯–G–A–B♭

Dominant chordChord tones (enharmonic spellings)
C7C E G B♭
E♭7E♭ G B♭ D♭
F♯7F♯ A♯ C♯ E
A7A C♯ E G

These roots are separated by minor thirds. The four dominant chords share the same octatonic collection, although the functions of individual notes change with each root. Similarly, Cdim7, E♭dim7, G♭dim7 and Adim7 contain the same four pitch classes, with different spellings and possible resolutions.

The whole-tone collection contains dominant seventh chords with raised fifths on each of its six pitches (when spelled appropriately), and the augmented scale contains major seventh and augmented major seventh possibilities on roots separated by major thirds.

A useful distinction: Sharing a scale does not make chords interchangeable in every musical context. Bass motion, voicing and resolution still matter. Symmetry gives us a way to discover related sounds, not a rule for substituting chords.

7. Choosing a scale for improvisation

Whole-tone over C7♯5

C–D–E–F♯–G♯–B♭

Contains C, E, G♯ and B♭, with D as 9 and F♯ as ♯11. There is no natural fifth. Try a short ascending phrase and resolve E to F or B♭ to A when C7 moves to F major.

Half–whole over C7

C–D♭–E♭–E–F♯–G–A–B♭

Contains the ordinary dominant seventh chord plus ♭9, ♯9, ♯11 and natural 13. Compare this with the altered scale, which lacks the natural fifth and natural thirteenth.

Whole–half over Cdim7

C–D–E♭–F–G♭–A♭–A–B

Alternate chord tones and neighbouring scale tones. Try moving from Cdim7 to D♭ major: C rises to D♭, E♭ may move to F, and G♭ may move to F or A♭.

Augmented over Cmaj7 or Cmaj7♯5

C–E♭–E–G–G♯–B

Both natural and raised fifths are present, as are minor and major thirds. These contrasts offer expressive tensions, but sustained notes may clash with a particular voicing. Explore short motifs rather than treating every pitch as equally stable.

A note on chord–scale thinking:

As we discussed in Jazz Theory 3, choosing scales for every chord can become complicated very quickly. The point is not to calculate transpositions at extraordinary speed. These collections give us a mechanism to hear and internalise new tonal colours.

Learning how experienced improvisers actually connect chords is better served by listening, transcribing and analysing solos. In forthcoming lessons we will explore how melodic direction, phrasing, rhythmic placement and voice leading make these colours meaningful in real music.

8. Listening and improvisation exercises

  1. Play C whole-tone and then C major over a C drone. Notice the absence of semitones and the perfect fifth in the whole-tone scale.
  2. Use the symmetry lab to transpose whole-tone by one and then two semitones. Repeat with diminished and augmented collections. Predict when each will return.
  3. Play a sustained C7 chord without added extensions. Compare C Mixolydian, C half–whole diminished and C altered. Listen to the natural 13 versus ♭13 and the different fifths.
  4. Improvise a short motif using C half–whole diminished. Repeat it three semitones higher and notice that every pitch still belongs to the same collection.
  5. Explore C augmented over a C drone and then over Cmaj7♯5. Notice how the accompaniment changes which pitches feel stable.
  6. Listen to Debussy’s Voiles. Try to recognise the character of the whole-tone collection without attempting to label every note.
  7. Choose one symmetrical collection and compose a short melody using repetition, space and rhythmic variation. Change the bass note and listen to the effect.

The goal is to develop a relationship with the sound of these collections and to understand how their internal structure opens up new possibilities for melody, harmony and composition.
