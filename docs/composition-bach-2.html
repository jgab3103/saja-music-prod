Understanding Harmony

Figured bass, inversions and harmonic progressions

In Part 1 we explored four independent melodic lines. Now we will begin with the bass and discover how the other voices create harmony above it. Return to Part 1.

1. What is figured bass?

Around 1600, the practice of basso continuo spread through Europe. Players of the organ, harpsichord or lute could improvise an accompaniment from a written bass line and numerical instructions. By Bach’s time, thoroughbass was also an important way of learning composition. Think of it as a musical shorthand, not unlike chord symbols in jazz, but organised around the bass rather than the chord root.

Numbers indicate intervals above the bass, normally using the key signature. A 6 above E in C major asks for C; the implied third is G. A sharp, flat or natural alters the indicated interval; an accidental alone alters the third. The figures do not prescribe exact octaves, doubling or voice allocation.

Exact mode uses the opening tonic, chord quality and inversion. Flexible mode ignores inversions, collapses repeated harmonies, skips unclassified sonorities and admits unambiguous seventh chords without a fifth. Transposition-independent mode additionally matches the same root movements and chord qualities at any pitch level. These are exploratory matches, not a full analysis of local key or harmonic function. Repeats are not expanded.

6. Composition exercises

Choose a short passage and use manuscript paper or a piano to explore alternative harmonisations.

  • Reduce the four voices to a succession of block chords. Identify the roots and inversions.
  • Keep the soprano and chord roots, but write an alternative bass using different chord inversions.
  • Choose four successive chords and change one chord quality. Listen to the effect on the phrase.
  • Write a short progression of your own, then distribute its notes across four independent voices. Compare it with Bach's writing.

Consider which harmonies feel stable, which create tension and which invite further movement. There is no need to reproduce Bach's exact choices.
