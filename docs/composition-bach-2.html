Figured Bass in Bach's Chorales

Figured bass, inversions and harmonic progressions

In Part 1 we explored four independent melodic lines. Now we will begin with the bass and discover how the other voices create harmony above it. Return to Part 1.

1. What is figured bass?

Around 1600, the practice of basso continuo spread through Europe. Players of the organ, harpsichord or lute could improvise an accompaniment from a written bass line and numerical instructions. By Bach’s time, thoroughbass was also an important way of learning composition. Think of it as a musical shorthand, not unlike chord symbols in jazz, but organised around the bass rather than the chord root.

Numbers indicate intervals above the bass, normally using the key signature. A 6 above E in C major asks for C; the implied third is G. A sharp, flat or natural alters the indicated interval; an accidental alone alters the third. The figures do not prescribe exact octaves, doubling or voice allocation.

How to read a figured bass

Start with the written bass note, not the root of the chord. Count letter names upwards, including the bass as 1. In C major, a bass E with 6 gives E–G–C: G is a third above E and C is a sixth. This is a C major triad in first inversion. In a different key, the same figures may produce different chord qualities. Use the key signature first, then apply any accidentals in the figures.

Beyond the basic figures

Accidentals and chromatic harmony

An accidental without a number alters the third above the bass. For example, in A minor, a sharp beneath E calls for G♯ rather than G, giving E–G♯–B. An accidental beside a number alters that particular interval: over C in C major, ♯6 calls for A♯. A natural cancels an accidental, and a slashed figure or a plus sign commonly raises the indicated interval. Historical conventions vary, so consult the edition when necessary.

Suspensions and changing figures

Numbers stacked vertically describe simultaneous intervals. Numbers placed successively describe changes over time, often in one upper voice. Over C, a 4–3 suspension can move F to E while the bass stays on C; a 9–8 suspension can move D to C in the octave above. A 7–6 movement can likewise resolve down by step. These figures can describe melodic motion rather than a succession of independent chords. A continuation line may indicate a sustained interval or harmony; read it in context.

Compound intervals, doubling and spacing

Figures such as 9, 10 and 11 refer to compound intervals: a ninth is a second plus an octave, a tenth a third plus an octave, and an eleventh a fourth plus an octave. Basic figures usually leave octave placement and doubling to the performer. A triad in four-part writing needs one pitch doubled; choose a distribution that keeps each voice singable and avoids awkward motion. A seventh chord may sometimes omit its fifth, depending on context.

Figured bass versus Roman numerals

Figured bass tells us which intervals sound above the written bass. Roman numerals tell us the chord root and quality in relation to a tonal centre. In C major, E with 6 can be I6, but C with 6 can be vi6. The figure alone does not identify harmonic function. A cadential I6/4, for example, often behaves as a decoration of the dominant rather than an independent tonic.

A practical way to realise a bass

Read the key and bass, expand the abbreviation, apply accidentals, then choose the upper notes. Play the result. Move to the next bass note, keeping common tones where useful and seeking smooth melodic lines. Listen for the resolution of dissonances. There can be several musically convincing realisations of the same figures.

5. Composition exercises

Choose a short passage and use manuscript paper or a piano to explore alternative harmonisations.

  • Reduce the four voices to a succession of block chords. Identify the roots and inversions.
  • Keep the soprano and chord roots, but write an alternative bass using different chord inversions.
  • Choose four successive chords and change one chord quality. Listen to the effect on the phrase.
  • Write a short progression of your own, then distribute its notes across four independent voices. Compare it with Bach's writing.

Consider which harmonies feel stable, which create tension and which invite further movement. There is no need to reproduce Bach's exact choices.
