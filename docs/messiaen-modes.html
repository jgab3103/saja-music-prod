Messiaen’s Modes of Limited Transposition

Jazz Theory 5 · Theory, Harmony & Counterpoint · Symmetry · Composition · Improvisation

Explore the musical world of Olivier Messiaen, where symmetry, harmonic colour, rhythm and an extraordinary conception of musical time meet. Discover how seven pitch collections became part of one of the most distinctive compositional languages of the twentieth century.

1. From symmetrical scales to Messiaen

In Jazz Theory 4 – Symmetrical Scales, we discovered that some collections of notes reproduce themselves when transposed by less than an octave. Olivier Messiaen (1908–1992) made this property a central part of his musical language, developing a systematic approach to harmony and melody that he called the modes of limited transposition (modes à transpositions limitées).

These are not seven rotations of one parent scale, like the major or melodic minor modes. Each is a different family of pitch collections, defined by a repeating interval structure. Some contain familiar triads; others suggest dense, almost chromatic harmonies. Their significance lies not simply in which chords they fit, but in the kinds of musical worlds they make possible.

For a jazz musician, they offer a way to extend the study of tonal colour beyond conventional chord–scale relationships. For a composer, they provide a means of organising pitch without making every harmony subordinate to a tonic or a dominant.

Listen first: The purpose of this lesson is to discover sounds, not to memorise seven more scales or calculate transpositions in real time.

2. Historical context: a new musical language

At the beginning of the twentieth century, European composers were increasingly exploring alternatives to the functional harmonic language that had dominated much of the preceding two centuries. This did not mean that tonality suddenly disappeared. Rather, composers developed new ways of establishing coherence, including recurring sonorities, motivic development, modal collections and symmetrical structures.

Before Messiaen – late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

The whole-tone and octatonic collections were already present in music before Messiaen. Rimsky-Korsakov and Stravinsky explored octatonic resources; Debussy used whole-tone harmony to suspend ordinary expectations of resolution. Listen to Debussy’s Voiles (1909), where whole-tone material contrasts with pentatonic writing. Messiaen did not invent these collections; his contribution was to bring them into a broader, explicit compositional system.

1908–1931 – formation in France

Born in Avignon, Messiaen entered the Paris Conservatoire in 1919. His education included organ, improvisation and composition, with teachers including Marcel Dupré and Paul Dukas. In 1931 he became organist at the church of La Sainte-Trinité in Paris, a position he held for the rest of his life. His Catholic faith and his experience of organ improvisation were central to his artistic identity.

1935 – La Nativité du Seigneur

In the preface to this nine-movement organ cycle, Messiaen presented the seven modes. The work offers an early opportunity to hear his modal language alongside religious imagery, extended harmonies and unusual rhythmic structures. The modes were already practical compositional materials, not merely a theoretical proposal.

1940–1944 – captivity, the Quartet and a theoretical statement

During the Second World War, Messiaen was held as a prisoner of war at Stalag VIII-A. His Quatuor pour la fin du temps (Quartet for the End of Time) received its first performance there in January 1941. Its engagement with eternity, birdsong and unusual musical time exemplifies the larger aesthetic in which the modes operate. In 1944 he published Technique de mon langage musical, explaining his approach to pitch, harmony and rhythm in detail. The same year he composed the piano cycle Vingt Regards sur l’Enfant-Jésus.

1941–1978 – teaching and a changing musical language

At the Paris Conservatoire, Messiaen taught generations of composers, including Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen and Iannis Xenakis. His later music expanded its rhythmic, harmonic and orchestral resources, with birdsong becoming especially prominent from the 1950s. The modes remained part of his vocabulary but were never the entirety of his technique.

3. What does limited transposition mean?

Imagine the twelve pitch classes of equal temperament arranged on a circle. Transposing a collection by one semitone rotates every note by one position. Octave placement and enharmonic spelling are ignored when comparing the collections.

An ordinary major scale

C D E F G A B

Transpose by one semitone and the collection changes. Continue through all twelve semitone positions: each collection is different until the octave is reached.

A whole-tone scale

C D E F♯ G♯ A♯

Transpose by one semitone and every pitch class changes. Transpose by two and the original pitch classes return, although individual pitches have moved.

The whole-tone scale has only two distinct transpositions, rather than twelve. The same principle applies to each of Messiaen’s modes. A repeating cell divides the octave into equal spans; moving by one complete cell reproduces the collection.

For example, the octatonic scale repeats a cell spanning three semitones. Its four copies fill the octave. It therefore has three distinct transpositions and four transpositions (including zero) that leave the pitch-class set unchanged.

A distinction: Transposing the pitch collection is different from choosing a new tonal centre within that collection. The return of the same notes does not imply that a melody, bass line or chord voicing sounds identical.

4. The seven modes

Here are representative forms beginning on C. The numbers in the repeating cell indicate semitones, including the interval that leads into the next cell. Enharmonic note names are used for readability rather than to imply a tonal function.

Mode 1 – Whole-tone

C–D–E–F♯–A♭–B♭

Repeating cell: 2 · Distinct transpositions: 2

Six equally spaced notes; augmented triads and no perfect fifths.

Mode 2 – Octatonic

C–D♭–E♭–E–F♯–G–A–B♭

Repeating cell: 1–2 · Distinct transpositions: 3

Four minor-third-related centres; major and minor triads, dominant sevenths and diminished sevenths.

Mode 3 – Nine-note mode

C–D–E♭–E–F♯–G–A♭–B♭–B

Repeating cell: 2–1–1 · Distinct transpositions: 4

Three repeated cells spanning a major third; dense harmony and augmented-triad symmetry.

Mode 4 – Eight-note mode

C–D♭–D–F–F♯–G–A♭–B

Repeating cell: 1–1–3–1 · Distinct transpositions: 6

Two groups related by a tritone; chromatic clusters separated by larger gaps.

Mode 5 – Six-note mode

C–D♭–F–F♯–G–B

Repeating cell: 1–4–1 · Distinct transpositions: 6

A sparse collection with two major-third gaps; useful for open, angular sonorities.

Mode 6 – Eight-note mode

C–D–E–F–F♯–A♭–B♭–B

Repeating cell: 2–2–1–1 · Distinct transpositions: 6

Whole-tone fragments interrupted by adjacent semitones.

Mode 7 – Ten-note mode

C–D♭–D–E♭–E–F♯–G–A♭–A–B♭

Repeating cell: 1–1–1–2–1 · Distinct transpositions: 6

Almost chromatic; only two pitch classes omitted, a tritone apart.

Messiaen used the modes unevenly, with Modes 2 and 3 particularly prominent, and Modes 4 and 6 also important. Modes 1, 5 and 7 were less central to his mature practice. His catalogue is a compositional selection, not an exhaustive mathematical list of all symmetrical pitch-class sets. The augmented scale from the previous lesson, for instance, also has limited transposition.

6. Colour, faith and the charm of impossibilities

Messiaen’s interest in symmetry was not primarily mathematical for its own sake. In his theoretical writings he connected limited transposition with other structures that have built-in restrictions. He described this aesthetic as the charm of impossibilities: a musical operation appears to promise change, yet the underlying structure prevents it from producing an entirely new result.

Symmetry in pitch

Mode 2 transposed by a minor third returns to its original pitch classes. Movement has occurred without changing the available collection.

Symmetry in time

A non-retrogradable rhythm reads identically forwards and backwards. For example, durations of 2–3–5–3–2 units form a palindrome. Reversing their order produces the same rhythm.

The two ideas concern different operations: pitch transposition and temporal reversal. What unites them is the preservation of a structure under transformation. They offer possibilities for music in which the usual sense of departure and return can be suspended, or in which movement is experienced without a conventional destination.

For Messiaen, these possibilities had spiritual significance. His Catholic imagination was concerned with eternity, divine presence and the limits of ordinary human experience of time. The mathematical properties do not themselves prove or encode a theological meaning; rather, they supplied musical materials through which he could express those ideas.

Sound and colour

Messiaen described highly specific associations between musical sonorities and colours. Different transpositions of a mode could have different colour associations for him, and register and voicing also mattered. This was a personal perceptual and imaginative experience, not a universal colour chart that students are expected to reproduce.

Think of harmony here as something that can be contemplated in its own right. A chord may be sustained, repeated, juxtaposed with another colour or distributed across contrasting instrumental registers, rather than being heard solely as a step towards a cadence. Messiaen’s later music also employed other colour-chord systems and birdsong; the modes are one component of a larger language.

Further reading: Vincent P. Benitez – Aspects of Harmony in Messiaen’s Later Music.

7. Harmony, composition and improvisation

Mode 2 offers a particularly clear bridge from the previous jazz theory lessons. The C half–whole collection contains C7, E♭7, F♯7 and A7 (with enharmonic respelling). The same material can support several contrasting bass notes.

ChordChord tones (enharmonic where needed)
C7C E G B♭
E♭7E♭ G B♭ D♭
F♯7F♯ A♯ C♯ E
A7A C♯ E G

Instead of treating these only as dominant chords requiring resolution, try moving between their triads or seventh chords while retaining the same melodic collection. A repeated motif can acquire a different character simply because the bass moves.

Mode 3 contains, for example, the C augmented triad (C–E–G♯) and its major-third transpositions, which are the same pitch classes. It also contains C major (C–E–G) and E major (E–G♯–B). These familiar sonorities can coexist within a larger non-diatonic environment.

Compose within one collection

Choose Mode 4. Write a short melody, then build two or three contrasting chords from its notes. Use a pedal bass or silence to connect them. Avoid assuming that a dominant–tonic cadence is required.

Change colour without changing mode

Write a phrase in Mode 3, then transpose the collection by one semitone. Retain the rhythmic shape. Compare this with transposing by four semitones, which returns to the original collection.

Explore density

Compare Modes 5 and 7. Use the same rhythm and register, then vary the spacing of the notes. Notice how the sparse and nearly chromatic collections offer different melodic possibilities.

Combine pitch and time

Choose a mode and play a short phrase using the palindromic rhythm 2–3–5–3–2. Repeat it with different pitches. Discover how rhythmic identity can persist while harmony changes.

A note for improvisers: There is no requirement to assign one of these modes to every chord in a jazz standard. Explore them as sounds first, over a drone or a sustained harmony. As discussed in Jazz Theory 3, learning to negotiate rapidly changing harmony is often better served by listening to and transcribing solos than by continually calculating scale choices.

8. Listening in context

Listen to these works as musical experiences before trying to identify a mode. Revisit a short passage and investigate its notes afterwards. Not every passage in these works is governed by a single mode.

Debussy – Voiles (1909)

Listen for the floating whole-tone sonority and the contrast with pentatonic material. This offers a useful historical precursor to Messiaen’s approach.

Messiaen – La Nativité du Seigneur (1935)

Explore the organ’s changing registers and sustained harmonies. In movements such as La Vierge et l’Enfant and Les Bergers, consider the relationship between modal colour and texture.

Messiaen – Quatuor pour la fin du temps (1940–41)

Begin with Liturgie de cristal, where recurring piano and cello patterns coexist with birdsong-like lines. Compare the sense of time in Louange à l’Éternité de Jésus. Listen for the difference between harmonic movement and the experience of musical time.

Messiaen – Vingt Regards sur l’Enfant-Jésus (1944)

Compare the spacious opening of Regard du Père with the energy of Regard de l’Esprit de joie. Recurrent themes and contrasting textures show how pitch organisation operates alongside other compositional ideas.

Messiaen – Couleurs de la Cité céleste (1963)

Listen for the changing instrumental colours, chordal blocks and contrasts. This later work provides a way to explore how his harmonic language developed beyond the early catalogue of modes.

Why these ideas still matter

Messiaen demonstrates that a coherent musical language can emerge from the internal relationships of a pitch collection, without requiring all musical events to be explained by functional harmony. Symmetry becomes a creative constraint, rather than a restriction on imagination. The modes also connect mathematics, listening, composition, improvisation and musical perception in a particularly direct way.

For students, the most important outcome is not the ability to recite all seven formulas. It is the ability to hear a new sonority, recognise its possibilities, and use it intentionally in a musical phrase or composition.
