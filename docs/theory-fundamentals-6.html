Theory Fundamentals · Part 6

Triad Inversions

Listening · Rearranging notes · Treble-clef notation · Interactive practice

A C major triad contains C, E and G. But does C always have to be the lowest note? What happens if we put E at the bottom, or G? Let's move the notes around and listen.

1. The same chord, three arrangements

There are three ways to arrange a triad so that each of its notes takes a turn as the lowest note. These are called root position, first inversion and second inversion.

All three examples contain C, E and G. Their order changes, and so does the lowest note, or bass note. The chord is still C major.

An inversion is determined by the lowest sounding note. Root position has the root at the bottom. First inversion has the third at the bottom. Second inversion has the fifth at the bottom. The bass is not necessarily the lowest note in a single hand or on a single staff: it is the lowest note sounding in the entire musical texture.

Quick check 1

Which note is in the bass?

A C major chord is written E–G–C, from lowest to highest. Which inversion is it?

2. Move the lowest note up an octave

Starting with a root-position triad, move the bottom note up an octave. You have made first inversion. Move the new bottom note up an octave again, and you have second inversion. One more move returns to root position an octave higher.

3. Compare all three inversions

Choose any major, minor, diminished or augmented triad. Each position is written on the treble staff. Listen to them one at a time. Notice that each version contains the same three pitch classes, even though some notes move to another octave.

Inversion comparison

4. Recognising inversions

You do not need to memorise dozens of individual chord shapes. Instead, find the chord's three notes, determine its root, and then check which note is lowest.

Root position

The root is the bass. In a close-position triad, the notes form a stack of two thirds on the staff.

First inversion

The third is the bass. In a close-position triad, there is a third above the bass, then a fourth.

Second inversion

The fifth is the bass. In a close-position triad, there is a fourth above the bass, then a third.

Remember

Inversion depends on the bass note, not simply on the spacing between notes. Open voicings can still be in any inversion.
