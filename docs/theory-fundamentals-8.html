Theory Fundamentals · Part 8

Beyond Major and Minor: More Scales, More Chords

New scale patterns · Unusual sounds · Seventh-chord construction · Listening

We have built seventh chords using major and minor scales. But the collection of notes we choose affects the chords we can make. What happens if we change the notes of the scale?

There are many other scales. Some contain seven notes, just like major and minor; others contain six or eight notes. They give us new colours for melody and harmony. Let's explore them, and use them to discover more seventh chords.

A scale is a set of pitch choices, not a rule about which chords you must play. You can stack alternate scale notes to make chords, or select particular notes from the scale to make other structures. These are related approaches, but they are not the same thing.

1. A small change creates a new scale

Earlier, we compared major, natural minor and harmonic minor. Now try harmonic major: a major scale with a lowered sixth. That one change creates a different interval pattern and different seventh chords.

Compare C major and C harmonic major

C major: C–D–E–F–G–A–B. C harmonic major: C–D–E–F–G–A♭–B. Only degree 6 changes, but the distances around it change too.

4. Construct seventh chords by changing their intervals

There is another way to think about chord construction. Choose a third, a fifth and a seventh above the root. Changing any one of these notes gives you a different four-note sound.

For example, C–E–G–B♭ is C7. Lower its fifth from G to G♭ and it becomes C7♭5. Or start with C–E♭–G♭–B♭, a half-diminished seventh, and lower the seventh again to B𝄫 (the same sounding pitch as A): now it is C°7.

5. Where can we find those sounds?

A scale may contain every note needed for a chord even if those chord tones do not appear at positions 1–3–5–7 in that scale. That is particularly useful for dominant chords with altered fifths.

Spelling matters. C–E–G♭–B♭ is a dominant seventh with a lowered fifth (C7♭5). The same sounding pitch as G♭ can be called F♯, but its role in the chord is different. We will keep the chord notation aligned with the interval being described.

8. Why do we construct scales and chords?

We construct scales by choosing patterns of tones and semitones within an octave. Change the pattern, and you change the collection of notes available to you. Some scales have five or six notes, others seven or eight.

We construct familiar seventh chords by stacking thirds: the root, third, fifth and seventh. We can also alter those chord tones to create new qualities. Different scales reveal different combinations of notes, and each one offers possibilities for melodies and harmony.

The possibilities are enormous — but the goal is not to memorise every possible combination. It is to understand the relationships between notes and, most importantly, to hear what you create.
