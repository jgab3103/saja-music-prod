Understanding the Modes of the Melodic Minor Scale

Jazz Theory 3 · Theory, Harmony & Counterpoint · Scales · Improvisation

Explore a family of scales that lies at the heart of modern jazz harmony. From the minor-major seventh sound to Lydian dominant and the altered scale, discover how melodic minor provides new ways of shaping melodies over familiar chords.

In this lesson

1. What is the melodic minor scale?

In Jazz Theory 1 and Jazz Theory 2, we explored how different selections of notes, and different tonal centres within those selections, create distinct musical characters.

The melodic minor scale, as used in jazz, is natural minor with both the sixth and seventh degrees raised. Compared with the major scale, only the third is lowered.

A natural minor

A–B–C–D–E–F–G

1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7

A harmonic minor

A–B–C–D–E–F–G♯

1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 7

A melodic minor

A–B–C–D–E–F♯–G♯

1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 7

A major

A–B–C♯–D–E–F♯–G♯

1 2 3 4 5 6 7

Raising F to F♯ removes the augmented second between F and G♯ in harmonic minor. The upper part of melodic minor now moves smoothly by a whole step and then a half step towards A. Its minor third retains the minor character while the upper notes resemble a major scale.

Interval pattern: whole – half – whole – whole – whole – whole – half.
In jazz we normally use this same collection ascending and descending. Listen in the Sound of Scales Lab.

2. The seven modes of melodic minor

Each of the seven notes of A melodic minor can become a tonal centre. As before, the notes remain identical; the intervals relative to the tonic change. A different starting note alone does not establish a mode.

A–B–C–D–E–F♯–G♯

Minor with natural sixth and major seventh

2. B Dorian ♭2

B–C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A

Minor with ♭2 and natural 6

3. C Lydian augmented

C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A–B

Raised fourth and fifth

4. D Lydian dominant

D–E–F♯–G♯–A–B–C

Dominant seventh with ♯11

5. E Mixolydian ♭6

E–F♯–G♯–A–B–C–D

Dominant seventh with ♭13 and natural 9

6. F♯ Locrian ♮2

F♯–G♯–A–B–C–D–E

7. G♯ Altered

G♯–A–B–C–D–E–F♯

Altered dominant tensions

Where to begin: Melodic minor, Lydian dominant, Locrian ♮2 and the altered scale are particularly valuable in jazz. The remaining modes offer additional colours; there is no need to devote equal practice time to all seven.

3. A brief historical and musical context

Minor-key tonal practice

Composers have long varied the sixth and seventh degrees in minor keys according to melodic direction and harmony. Raising the seventh creates a leading note, while raising the sixth can smooth the approach to it.

Baroque and Classical music

The conventional teaching model presents melodic minor with raised sixth and seventh ascending, and natural minor descending. Actual compositions are more flexible: the harmony and melodic line determine which notes are appropriate, in either direction.

Twentieth-century jazz

Jazz musicians increasingly treated melodic minor as a fixed pitch collection available in both directions. Modern chord–scale teaching systematised its rotations as resources for minor-major seventh, half-diminished, augmented and dominant harmony.

Contemporary improvisation

The collection is particularly useful because several of its modes supply extensions that ordinary major-scale modes do not. Musicians can explore these sounds over a single chord or employ them briefly to intensify a resolution within a progression.

4. Understanding the character of each mode

Comparing parallel modes on the same tonic makes their individual sounds much easier to recognise.

Melodic minor vs harmonic minor

A harmonic minor: A–B–C–D–E–F–G♯
A melodic minor: A–B–C–D–E–F♯–G♯

The natural sixth gives a smoother upper register and supplies a natural 13 over minor-major seventh harmony.

Dorian vs Dorian ♭2

B Dorian: B–C♯–D–E–F♯–G♯–A
B Dorian ♭2: B–C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A

The lowered second creates a Phrygian-like tension, while the natural sixth retains a Dorian colour. Also called Phrygian ♮6.

Lydian vs Lydian augmented

C Lydian: C–D–E–F♯–G–A–B
C Lydian augmented: C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A–B

The raised fifth changes the tonic triad into an augmented chord; the raised fourth remains available as ♯11.

Mixolydian vs Lydian dominant

D Mixolydian: D–E–F♯–G–A–B–C
D Lydian dominant: D–E–F♯–G♯–A–B–C

Raising the fourth creates the distinctive ♯11 colour while preserving the dominant seventh chord. Also called the acoustic or overtone scale.

Mixolydian vs Mixolydian ♭6

E Mixolydian: E–F♯–G♯–A–B–C♯–D
E Mixolydian ♭6: E–F♯–G♯–A–B–C–D

Lowering the sixth gives a ♭13 while retaining a natural ninth. Compare this with Phrygian dominant, which also lowers the second.

Locrian vs Locrian ♮2

F♯ Locrian: F♯–G–A–B–C–D–E
F♯ Locrian ♮2: F♯–G♯–A–B–C–D–E

Raising the second provides a natural ninth over a half-diminished chord. Also called Locrian ♯2, meaning raised relative to ordinary Locrian.

Mixolydian vs altered

G♯ Mixolydian: G♯–A♯–B♯–C♯–D♯–E♯–F♯
G♯ altered: G♯–A–B–C–D–E–F♯

The altered scale retains the root, major third (C, enharmonically B♯) and minor seventh. It supplies ♭9, ♯9, ♭5/♯11 and ♯5/♭13, rather than a natural ninth, fifth or thirteenth.

Scale names vary across teaching systems. The altered scale is also called Super Locrian; it is not the same as Ultralocrian from harmonic minor.

6. Tonic chords and harmonic function

Stacking the first, third, fifth and seventh scale degrees produces the following diatonic chords. They all belong to A melodic minor.

B Dorian ♭2BmBm7
C Lydian augmentedCaugCmaj7♯5
D Lydian dominantDD7
E Mixolydian ♭6EE7
F♯ Locrian ♮2F♯dimF♯m7♭5
G♯ AlteredG♯dimG♯m7♭5
An important exception in jazz practice: Although the literal seventh mode yields G♯m7♭5 when stacked in thirds, jazz musicians usually hear it over G♯7alt instead. The fourth degree C is enharmonically B♯, the major third of G♯7. B is then heard as ♯9, D as ♭5, and E as ♭13. This interpretation is different from simply harmonising the mode in thirds.

D Lydian dominant may serve as a stable D7(♯11) modal centre, or as a dominant-quality chord within a progression. As in earlier lessons, chord quality and harmonic function are not the same thing.

7. Choosing a mode for improvisation

Start with the chord and the sound required, rather than trying to use every mode equally.

Minor tonic — Am(maj7) or Am6

A melodic minor: A–B–C–D–E–F♯–G♯

Both the natural sixth and major seventh are available. Explore F♯ against Am6 and G♯ against Am(maj7). A sustained G♯ can conflict with the G natural in Am7.

Dominant ♯11 — D7(♯11)

D Lydian dominant: D–E–F♯–G♯–A–B–C

Contains D, F♯, A and C, with G♯ as ♯11. This is a common choice for non-resolving dominant chords and tritone substitutions. For example, D♭7(♯11) can replace G7 on the way to Cmaj7.

Altered dominant — G7alt

G altered (from A♭ melodic minor): G–A♭–B♭–C♭–D♭–E♭–F

Hear C♭ as B, the major third. Over G7alt, A♭ is ♭9, B♭ is ♯9, D♭ is ♭5 and E♭ is ♭13. Try resolving B to C, F to E (or E♭ in minor), and A♭ to G. The unaltered fifth D is absent.

Half-diminished — Dm7♭5

D Locrian ♮2 (from F melodic minor): D–E–F–G–A♭–B♭–C

The natural ninth E is the distinguishing colour compared with ordinary D Locrian. This is a particularly useful option for the ii chord in a minor ii–V–i.

Augmented major seventh — Cmaj7♯5

C Lydian augmented: C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A–B

The raised fifth is a chord tone and F♯ supplies ♯11. Compare with Ionian ♯5 from harmonic minor, which has a natural fourth.

Other dominant and minor colours

E Mixolydian ♭6 offers a natural 9 and ♭13 over E7, particularly useful when approaching A minor.

B Dorian ♭2 offers ♭9 and natural 13 over Bm7. The close relationship between B and C requires careful listening, especially if the chord voicing already contains a natural ninth.

Remember: The chord symbol is a starting point, not a complete instruction. Listen to the voicing, melody, surrounding harmony and intended resolution.

8. Chord tones and melodic colour

Consider the different colours available over G7. Each collection supports the essential root, major third and minor seventh, but the additional pitches change the character of the improvisation.

ScaleNotesAvailable colours
G MixolydianG A B C D E F9, 11, 13
G Lydian dominantG A B C♯ D E F9, ♯11, 13
G Phrygian dominantG A♭ B C D E♭ F♭9, 11, ♭13
G alteredG A♭ B♭ B D♭ E♭ F♭9, ♯9, ♭5, ♭13

The natural fourth can rub against the major third, and altered tensions may clash with unaltered extensions in the accompaniment. The altered scale does not contain the natural fifth, so it is most useful when the voicing allows altered fifths or omits the fifth.

Applying melodic minor to a minor ii–V–i

In Jazz Theory 2 we explored using one harmonic minor collection across a minor ii–V–i. Another approach is to choose a different melodic minor collection for each chord. Consider Dm7♭5–G7alt–Cm(maj7).

ChordScale choiceParent melodic minor
Dm7♭5D Locrian ♮2F melodic minor
G7altG alteredA♭ melodic minor
Cm(maj7)C melodic minorC melodic minor

Rather than rapidly running three scales, try connecting nearby chord tones across the progression. For example, E over Dm7♭5 can move to E♭ over G7alt and remain as the minor third of Cm. A♭ can remain over the first two chords and resolve to G on the tonic.

A note on chord–scale thinking

As this ii–V–i example demonstrates, choosing a different scale for every chord can become very complicated very quickly. Even a short progression may involve several different parent scales, and the number of possible choices increases considerably as the harmony becomes more complex.

Understanding these relationships gives musicians access to a wider palette of tonal colours. However, the objective is not to learn to calculate intervals, identify parent scales and transpose them at extraordinary speed while improvising. Rather, these theoretical relationships provide a mechanism for discovering, hearing and gradually internalising different musical colours. Over time, these sounds can become part of a musician’s vocabulary without requiring conscious calculation.

The study of how to improvise over changing chords is often better served by listening to and transcribing the solos of experienced improvisers. Transcriptions allow students to discover how musicians actually connect chords through melodic lines, rhythm, phrasing, chromatic movement and voice leading, rather than simply assigning a scale to each chord. We will explore this approach in forthcoming lessons.

For now, treat the ii–V–i examples as listening and exploration exercises, rather than as a prescribed method for constructing an improvised solo.

Listening and improvisation exercises

  1. Play A harmonic minor and then A melodic minor over an A drone. Listen to the change from F to F♯.
  2. Improvise over D7, alternating D Mixolydian and D Lydian dominant. Emphasise G and G♯ and compare the sounds.
  3. Play G7alt followed by Cm. Explore short phrases using the altered tensions and resolve them into the tonic chord.
  4. Compare D Locrian and D Locrian ♮2 over Dm7♭5, listening particularly to E♭ and E.
  5. Practise Dm7♭5–G7alt–Cm(maj7) slowly using the three melodic minor collections above. Aim for smooth voice leading rather than scale patterns.
  6. Explore the other modes over their suggested chords, using short motifs, rhythmic variation and silence.

The goal is not simply to memorise seven additional scales, but to develop an awareness of how different pitch collections shape melodic colour and harmonic movement.
