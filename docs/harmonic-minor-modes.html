Understanding the Modes of the Harmonic Minor Scale

Jazz Theory 2 · Theory, Harmony & Counterpoint · Scales · Improvisation

Discover how raising a single note of the natural minor scale opens up a new family of seven modes, from the familiar sound of harmonic minor to Phrygian dominant and some less commonly explored harmonic colours.

1. What is the harmonic minor scale?

In Jazz Theory 1 – Understanding the Modes of the Major Scale, we explored how a collection of seven notes can acquire different characters depending on which note is established as the tonic. Here we apply the same idea to a different collection of pitches.

The harmonic minor scale is the natural minor scale with its seventh degree raised by a semitone.

A natural minor

A–B–C–D–E–F–G

1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7

A harmonic minor

A–B–C–D–E–F–G♯

1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 7

Raising G to G♯ creates a leading note a semitone below A. It also changes the chord built on E from Em to E major, or from Em7 to E7. This strengthens the movement from the dominant to the tonic in a minor key.

The interval from F to G♯ is an augmented second (three semitones). This unusual gap is an important part of the scale’s melodic character.

Interval pattern: whole – half – whole – whole – half – augmented second – half.
Listen to the difference between natural and harmonic minor in the Sound of Scales Lab.

2. The seven modes of harmonic minor

Just as with the major scale, each note of harmonic minor can become the tonal centre. The pitches remain the same, but the intervals measured from the new tonic change. Simply starting on a different note is not sufficient: bass, harmony and melodic resolution must help establish that note as home.

Where to begin: Harmonic minor and Phrygian dominant are especially useful starting points. The other five modes are valid musical materials, but they need not all receive equal practice time. Their usefulness depends on the music and harmony being explored.

3. A brief historical and musical context

Minor-key tonal harmony

As major–minor tonality developed, composers regularly raised the seventh degree of minor keys to create a leading note and a major dominant chord. The harmonic minor scale describes this practice. It is not a separate historical system of seven church modes.

Baroque and Classical music

In Bach and later tonal composers, the sixth and seventh degrees of minor keys are flexible. A piece in A minor may use G natural or G♯ and F natural or F♯ according to melodic direction and harmonic function. Harmonic minor is a useful model, not a rule that every note of a minor-key piece must follow.

Flamenco and related modal sounds

The fifth mode, Phrygian dominant, is often called Spanish Phrygian in modern scale teaching. Its flattened second and major third evoke sounds associated with flamenco. However, actual flamenco practice can use both minor and major thirds and other inflections; it is not reducible to one fixed seven-note scale. Related pitch patterns also occur in other musical traditions, which have their own tuning and melodic conventions.

Jazz and contemporary improvisation

In jazz, the harmonic minor collection is especially useful over dominant chords resolving to minor tonics. Its other modes offer particular colours for augmented, minor, major and diminished harmony. Rock, metal and film music also make use of the distinctive augmented second.

4. Understanding the character of each mode

Compare each mode with a familiar scale on the same tonic. This reveals the characteristic note changes rather than simply treating the mode as a rotation of A harmonic minor.

Harmonic minor vs Aeolian

A Aeolian: A–B–C–D–E–F–G
A harmonic minor: A–B–C–D–E–F–G♯

Listen to the pull of G♯ towards A and the gap between F and G♯.

Phrygian vs Phrygian dominant

E Phrygian: E–F–G–A–B–C–D
E Phrygian dominant: E–F–G♯–A–B–C–D

Raising the third changes the tonic chord from minor to major while preserving the flattened second and sixth. The F–G♯ augmented second is a defining melodic sound.

Locrian vs Locrian ♮6

B Locrian: B–C–D–E–F–G–A
B Locrian ♮6: B–C–D–E–F–G♯–A

The natural sixth adds a new colour without changing the half-diminished tonic seventh chord.

Ionian vs Ionian ♯5

C Ionian: C–D–E–F–G–A–B
C Ionian ♯5: C–D–E–F–G♯–A–B

The raised fifth creates an augmented tonic triad and a major seventh chord with a raised fifth.

Dorian vs Dorian ♯4

D Dorian: D–E–F–G–A–B–C
D Dorian ♯4: D–E–F–G♯–A–B–C

The raised fourth supplies a sharp-eleventh colour against a minor seventh chord. This mode is also called Romanian minor in some scale systems.

Lydian vs Lydian ♯2

F Lydian: F–G–A–B–C–D–E
F Lydian ♯2: F–G♯–A–B–C–D–E

The raised second introduces another tension alongside the characteristic raised fourth.

Locrian vs Ultralocrian

G♯ Locrian: G♯–A–B–C♯–D–E–F♯
G♯ Ultralocrian: G♯–A–B–C–D–E–F

The fourth and seventh are lowered further. The resulting diminished seventh chord is highly unstable in conventional tonal harmony.

These names are not entirely standardised. Ultralocrian is also called altered diminished; it should not be confused with the altered scale derived from melodic minor.

6. Tonic chords and harmonic function

Build a chord from the first, third, fifth and seventh notes of each mode. All seven chords below draw on exactly the same A harmonic minor collection.

A Harmonic minorAmAm(maj7)
B Locrian ♮6BdimBm7♭5
C Ionian ♯5CaugCmaj7♯5
D Dorian ♯4DmDm7
E Phrygian dominantEE7
F Lydian ♯2FFmaj7

In A minor, E7 generally functions as V7 and resolves to Am. But when E is established as home, E7 can instead be the tonic seventh chord of E Phrygian dominant. The chord quality stays the same while its function changes.

Two ways to hear E7: As V7 in A minor, it creates expectation of resolution. As I7 in E Phrygian dominant, it can provide a sustained modal environment for improvisation.

Some modes, especially Ultralocrian, are harder to establish as stable tonic environments. Their chords are often more useful as passing or resolving harmonies.

7. Choosing a mode for improvisation

Rather than learning all seven modes as equally common chord–scale choices, begin with the harmonic situations in which the collection is most useful.

Minor tonic: Am or Am(maj7)

A harmonic minor: A–B–C–D–E–F–G♯

Am(maj7) contains A, C, E and G♯. Harmonic minor includes all four. Over a plain Am triad, G♯ can act as a leading note or a sustained major seventh colour. Over Am7, G♯ may conflict with the chord’s G natural.

Dominant resolving to minor: E7(♭9,♭13)

E Phrygian dominant: E–F–G♯–A–B–C–D

E7 contains E, G♯, B and D. F adds ♭9 and C adds ♭13. Try resolving G♯ to A, D to C and F to E when the harmony moves to Am.

Half-diminished: Bm7♭5

B Locrian ♮6: B–C–D–E–F–G♯–A

In Bm7♭5–E7–Am, all three chords can be explored using the same A harmonic minor collection, with emphasis on the current chord tones. Other scale choices are also possible, particularly over the ii chord.

Minor seventh: Dm7

D Dorian ♯4: D–E–F–G♯–A–B–C

Explore G♯ as a ♯11 and B as a 13 over Dm7. Compare the sound with ordinary Dorian.

Augmented major seventh: Cmaj7♯5

C Ionian ♯5: C–D–E–F–G♯–A–B

G♯ is a chord tone. F may create tension against E; use it thoughtfully. Lydian augmented from melodic minor is another option, with a raised fourth.

Major seventh and diminished seventh

F Lydian ♯2 can colour Fmaj7 with ♯9 and ♯11. G♯ Ultralocrian contains G♯dim7 (G♯–B–D–F), useful when that chord resolves to Am. The diminished scale is another commonly used option over diminished seventh harmony.

Remember: A chord symbol alone does not determine the scale. Chord extensions, the melody, harmonic function and the desired sound all matter.

8. Chord tones and melodic colour

Consider E Mixolydian and E Phrygian dominant over E7. Both contain E, G♯, B and D, but their additional notes differ.

E MixolydianE Phrygian dominant
Chord tonesE G♯ B DE G♯ B D
Additional notesF♯ A C♯F A C
Colour9, 11, 13♭9, 11, ♭13

Changing just two notes creates a substantially different melodic environment while leaving the basic chord unchanged. The natural fourth (A) can rub against G♯ in either scale; listen to how it behaves in context.

Listening and improvisation exercises

  1. Play A natural minor over an A drone, then raise G to G♯. Listen for the leading note and augmented second.
  2. Play E Phrygian over an E drone, then raise G to G♯. Introduce an E major chord and explore the new relationship.
  3. Improvise over a sustained E7 chord, alternating E Mixolydian and E Phrygian dominant. Emphasise the second and sixth degrees.
  4. Play Bm7♭5–E7–Am slowly. Use A harmonic minor throughout, but aim for the notes of each chord as the harmony changes.
  5. Explore the less familiar modes over their tonic chords, using short phrases and sustained characteristic notes rather than simply running up and down the scale.

The goal is to hear how a change in the selection of pitches changes the musical character, and to make those choices deliberately in improvisation.
