Theory Fundamentals · Part 2

Minor Scales

Music theory · Beginner · Interactive lesson

In Part 1, we learnt that the pattern of tones and semitones determines a scale. The major scale uses one pattern. Minor scales use different patterns — and they sound different too.

Two skills to practise alongside this lesson

Keep learning to recognise notes on the piano keyboard using Find the Piano Key, and start practising notes on the treble clef with Find That Note!. We will introduce staff notation gradually. For now, we can understand minor scales entirely on the piano.

1. A different pattern makes a different scale

There are twelve notes in an octave. Moving to the next key — black or white — is one semitone (S). Moving two keys, with exactly one key between them, is one tone (T).

A scale is made by starting on a note and following a particular pattern of these distances. The major scale has one pattern; the natural minor scale has another.

Compare the patterns

We number scale notes 1 to 7. The next note is 8 — the tonic again, one octave higher. The curved arrows show the distance from one note to the next.

Major: T – T – S – T – T – T – S
TTSTTTS
Natural minor: T – S – T – T – S – T – T
TSTTSTT

Both patterns add up to twelve semitones — one octave. The semitones occur in different places.

2. Build a natural minor scale

Start on A. Move up a tone to B, then a semitone to C. Keep following T–S–T–T–S–T–T and you will reach A again, one octave higher.

A natural minor, note by note

Every note has a scale degree. The numbers remain the same when you change the starting note — it is the distances between them that make this a minor scale.

4. Natural, harmonic and melodic minor

We often use three forms of the minor scale. They share the same tonic and the same first five notes. What happens to the sixth and seventh notes makes a difference to their sound.

Natural minor

No raised notes beyond those required by the key's natural minor scale. Pattern: T–S–T–T–S–T–T.

Harmonic minor

Raise degree 7 of natural minor by one semitone. This produces a striking three-semitone gap between degrees 6 and 7.

Melodic minor

In classical usage, raise degrees 6 and 7 on the way up. On the way down, return to natural minor.

For example, A natural minor has G. A harmonic minor raises G to G♯. In ascending A melodic minor, F becomes F♯ and G becomes G♯. Listen to all three with the explorer above — then try changing the tonic.
