Composition Study: Bach Chorales · Part 4

Counterpoint: The Movement of Voices

Contrary, similar, parallel and oblique motion

In the first three parts of this series, you explored Bach's four-part writing, learnt to read figured bass and began to describe harmonic relationships using Roman numerals. Return to Part 3.

We now turn our attention from the vertical relationships between notes to the horizontal movement of individual voices. A chorale can be understood as a succession of chords, but it is also four melodies unfolding simultaneously. Counterpoint is concerned with how these melodies work together while retaining a sense of independence.

Our starting point will be two voices. Once you can hear and describe how two lines move in relation to one another, it becomes easier to understand the more complex relationships in four-part writing.

1. Four types of contrapuntal motion

Look at the direction of each voice from one note to the next. Play the short examples below, first together and then independently. These are simple teaching examples, not quotations from Bach. The upper voice is burgundy and the lower voice is blue.

Parallel motion is a particular kind of similar motion: the voices move in the same direction and retain the same generic interval (for example, successive thirds, even if major and minor thirds alternate). If both voices repeat their notes, there is no pitch motion. Compare voices at successive sounding moments, including sustained notes, rather than simply matching their written note attacks.

2. The independence of voices

Not all intervals have the same effect. Thirds and sixths are often used in parallel in tonal counterpoint, while consecutive parallel perfect fifths and octaves between the same two voices are generally avoided in Bach-style chorale writing. These perfect intervals can make the voices sound more like a single reinforced line.

Listen to the two examples below. Both use the same lower melody. The first doubles it at the octave; the second moves in thirds. Both have parallel motion, but the effect is different. Neither example is a complete contrapuntal phrase.

Contrary motion is not automatically good, nor is parallel motion automatically bad. The context, the intervals, the rhythm and the melodic shape all matter. We will explore consonance and dissonance more carefully in the next part.

3. Explore the movement of voices in Bach

Return to three short passages from Part 3. The commentary points to a few individual moments rather than analysing every note. Select two voices, listen to the passage, and trace their directions on the score. Then change the pair of voices and listen again.

Jesu, der du meine Seele · BWV 354

Measures 1–2

Soprano and bass

In measure 1, from beat 2 to beat 3, the soprano moves from F5 to C5 while the bass rises from D♭3 to F3: contrary motion. From beat 1 to its second half, the soprano sustains F5 while the bass moves from B♭2 to C3: oblique motion.

Listen to the bass throughout this passage. How does its eighth-note movement interact with the more sustained hymn melody?

Kommt her zu mir, spricht Gottes Sohn · BWV 74/8

Measures 3–4

Soprano and bass

In measure 3, from beat 2 to beat 3, the soprano moves from B4 to C5 and the bass from E3 to A3. Both rise, but the interval changes: similar motion. From beat 3 to its second half, C5 rises to D5 while A3 rises to B3: parallel tenths.

Now isolate the tenor and alto. Notice how the repeated tenor E4 in measure 3 allows the alto to move around it.

Christ lag in Todesbanden · BWV 278

Measures 3–4

Alto and tenor

In measure 3, from beat 3 to its second half, the alto moves from B3 to D♯4 and the tenor from E4 to F♯4. The tenor is above the alto at both moments: an example of voice crossing. In measure 4, from beat 1 to its second half, the alto moves from E4 to D♯4 while the tenor sustains B3: oblique motion.

Listen to the inner voices and then restore all four. Can you follow each line even when the voices cross?

5. Composition and analysis exercises

Work on manuscript paper and play or sing your work. At this stage, the emphasis is on listening, melodic shape and awareness of the relationship between voices.

  1. Identify motion. Copy two measures of a chorale onto manuscript paper. Choose two voices, mark their intervals and label each transition as contrary, similar, parallel, oblique or stationary. Repeat with another pair.
  2. Write four short examples. In a key of your choice, write two four-note melodies demonstrating each type of motion. Play both lines separately and together.
  3. Compose against a given melody. Write C4–D4–E4–F4–G4–F4–D4–C4 in quarter notes across two bars. Compose a second line beneath it using mostly thirds and sixths, with a mixture of contrary and oblique motion where possible. Aim for a consonant beginning and ending.
  4. Explore alternatives. Use the same melody to write a contrasting second line. Try changing its rhythm, holding a note while the other voice moves, or changing the melodic direction. Compare the two versions by ear.
  5. Return to Bach. Choose two measures, mute one voice and compose a replacement. Listen to your version with the other voices and consider whether the new line is singable and supports the harmony.

These are preliminary experiments, not a complete set of counterpoint rules. Keep your written work for the next lesson, when we will consider how consonance and dissonance shape these relationships.
