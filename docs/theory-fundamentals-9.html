Theory Fundamentals · Part 9

Basic Chord Progressions

Diatonic chords · Listening · Four-chord sequences · Melody composition

We can construct scales, triads and seventh chords. Now let's put those chords together. When chords follow one another, they form a chord progression.

Different orders produce different musical feelings. There is no single correct order. In this lesson, we'll experiment with chords from one scale, listen to familiar sequences and begin writing melodies over them.

1. Seven chords from one scale

Remember: a triad is constructed by choosing every other note from a scale. In C major, we can build one triad on each of its seven degrees. Look at all seven on the same treble staff. Choose a chord to hear it.

2. Put four chords into a sequence

Choose four chords from the same scale. Each one will sound for the same amount of time. Listen to the complete sequence, then change just one chord and compare the result.

3. Discover familiar patterns

Some chord sequences occur frequently in Western popular and classical music. Try these examples, then alter them. For now, the Roman numerals simply tell us which scale degrees the chords come from.

Try a progression

Roman numerals: upper-case letters usually indicate major triads; lower-case letters indicate minor triads; ° indicates diminished. They describe chord quality and degree, not just a particular root note.

Listen and reflect

Try I–IV–V–I. Then try I–V–vi–IV. Which one gives you a stronger feeling of arriving home? What happens if you start and finish on a different chord?

There is no graded answer: this is about noticing how different sequences sound.

4. What happens if we add sevenths?

In Part 7, we learned to build seventh chords by stacking one more third. We can use those same four-note chords inside a progression. Keep your four chord choices and switch between triads and seventh chords to hear the change.

5. Write a melody over your progression

We have previously made melodies using notes from scales. Now try making a melody while the chords play underneath. Choose one of the seven scale degrees for each note, then listen to the combined result. Sometimes a melody note belongs to the chord sounding underneath it; sometimes it creates a different colour.

Melody composition lab

Add up to eight notes. Two melody notes will be played over each chord. You can leave the melody unfinished and add to it later.

Select a scale degree to begin composing.

Challenge: make two contrasting eight-note melodies over the same chords. Try mostly stepwise motion in one and wider leaps in the other. Then write your favourite on manuscript paper.

6. Why might we need inversions?

Look at a root-position progression such as C–F–G–C. Each chord is built upward from its root. This can make the individual notes jump a considerable distance as the chords change.

What if we rearranged each chord so that its notes could move smaller distances? We already know how to invert triads. Seventh chords can be inverted too. This is the beginning of voice leading: thinking about the movement of individual notes from one chord to another.

Final creative challenge

Compose your own four-chord progression and eight-note melody. Save the chord names and write both the progression and melody on manuscript paper. Try it in a second key, or switch to seventh chords and listen to how the sound changes.
