Harmonic Function and the II–V–I

Jazz Theory 7 · Theory, Harmony & Counterpoint · Harmonic progressions · Repertoire

In the previous lessons we explored different pitch collections and how to construct jazz chords. Now we begin to investigate how chords move in relation to one another. The emphasis changes from learning theoretical materials in isolation to recognising musical relationships through the study of repertoire.

In this lesson

1. Why progressions recur2. Harmonic function3. The major ii–V–I4. The minor ii–V–i5. Voice leading and harmonic rhythm6. A progression in many different keys7. Learning repertoire8. Listening and practice

1. Why progressions recur

At first, a collection of jazz standards can appear overwhelming. Each tune may contain dozens of chords, and each chord can have numerous extensions, alterations and voicings. However, many standards draw on a shared vocabulary of harmonic movements. The same progression can reappear in another key, with different harmonic rhythm, or as part of a different formal structure.

This is not simply a teaching convention. Corpus research by Shanahan and Broze (2012), based on jazz chord sequences, identifies ii–V–I as a particularly persistent harmonic schema. The Jazz Harmony Treebank (Harasim and colleagues, 2020) provides hierarchical analyses of 150 standards, showing how chords can prepare other chords or extend a harmonic region. These studies help explain why recognising groups of chords is useful. They do not imply that every jazz composition follows the same syntax.

From this point onwards, learning repertoire is essential. The aim is not to accumulate increasingly sophisticated theoretical knowledge before beginning to play music. Learn tunes alongside these lessons. The repertoire is where harmonic relationships become meaningful musical experiences.

2. Harmonic function

Harmonic function describes the role a chord plays in a particular musical context. Chord quality tells us how a chord is constructed; function tells us what it is doing. A dominant seventh chord need not always function as a dominant, and a major seventh chord need not always be a tonic.

Tonic

Cmaj7

A point of arrival or relative stability in C major.

Predominant

Dm7

Often prepares the dominant; IV can also serve this role.

Dominant

G7

Often creates expectation of a return to C.

Context matters

C7 – Fmaj7

C7 can function as a dominant of F, even within a piece in C major.

Roman numerals describe the root relative to a tonal centre. Uppercase numerals indicate major triads and lowercase numerals minor triads; additional symbols specify seventh-chord quality. Review intervals, triads, seventh chords and Jazz Theory 6 – Chord Construction.

3. The major ii–V–I

Build seventh chords on degrees 2, 5 and 1 of C major, using alternate notes of the scale.

FunctionChordNotesFormula
ii7Dm7D F A C1 ♭3 5 ♭7
V7G7G B D F1 3 5 ♭7
Imaj7Cmaj7C E G B1 3 5 7

The roots D–G–C move by ascending fourths (equivalently descending fifths). The tritone B–F in G7 can resolve to C–E, producing a characteristic sense of arrival. Jazz voicings also frequently retain B as the major seventh of Cmaj7.

One collection, three chords: All three chords belong to C major. It is possible to improvise using the C major collection throughout, hearing how the notes relate differently to each chord. There is no need to calculate three separate modes in real time.

4. The minor ii–V–i

In C minor, the ii chord is normally half-diminished, and the dominant generally contains the raised seventh of the key, B natural. This produces a strong leading note towards C.

FunctionChordNotes
iiø7Dm7♭5D F A♭ C
V7(♭9)G7(♭9)G B D F A♭
iCmC E♭ G

The tonic may appear as Cm6, Cm(maj7) or Cm7, depending on the composition. Compare the sound of E and A in the major example with E♭ and A♭ in the minor one. C harmonic minor contains the notes of the example above, though actual melodies and chord voicings often draw on other collections too. Revisit Jazz Theory 2 and Jazz Theory 3 for alternative colours.

5. Voice leading and harmonic rhythm

Rather than jumping from one root-position chord to another, listen to how the inner voices connect. The third and seventh often provide a particularly clear outline of the harmonic movement.

VoiceDm7G7Cmaj7
UpperC (7th)B (3rd)B (7th)
LowerF (3rd)F (7th)E (3rd)
BassDGC

These two upper lines move by semitone or remain stationary. Try singing each line separately while playing the bass notes. Then compare a more conventional resolution of B upwards to C.

Slow harmonic rhythm

| Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 | Cmaj7 |

One chord per bar, with two bars of tonic.

Faster harmonic rhythm

| Dm7 G7 | Cmaj7 |

Two chords in the first bar. The function is unchanged, but the musical phrasing differs.

Not every ii–V resolves to the anticipated tonic. In real tunes a destination may be delayed, redirected or implied. Avoid forcing every sequence into the same interpretation.

6. A progression in many different keys

Use this explorer to hear the same harmonic relationship in different keys. Choose major or minor, a tempo, and either root-position chords or compact guide-tone voicings. Play along, sing the bass or practise short melodic phrases. The generated examples are original exercises, not backing tracks for particular standards.

Ready.

Practise in a few keys at first, then expand gradually. Transposition is a way to recognise a familiar relationship in a new register or tonal centre, not a race to memorise all twelve keys.

7. Learning repertoire

There is no substitute for knowing a body of music. Repertoire provides the melodies, forms, harmonic rhythms and expressive contexts that an isolated exercise cannot reproduce. Begin with a small number of standards and learn them deeply. Return to them as new concepts are introduced.

Autumn Leaves

Study the movement between relative major and minor. Locate the major and minor cadences. Listen to Cannonball Adderley's recording on Somethin’ Else.

Tune Up

Listen for successive major ii–V–I units in different keys. Try Miles Davis's recording on Cookin’ with the Miles Davis Quintet.

Blue Bossa

Compare minor-key and major-key cadential regions. Listen to Joe Henderson's recording on Page One.

Satin Doll

Listen for successive ii–V groups and notice that not all of them resolve immediately. Explore a Duke Ellington recording.

What does it mean to learn a standard?

  1. Listen to several recordings, including the original song or an early recording where possible.
  2. Learn to sing the melody and play it on the instrument, without relying on the page.
  3. Identify the form and sing or play the bass movement.
  4. Use a reliable lead sheet to identify chord qualities, tonal centres and recurring harmonic units. Compare against recordings, since charts can differ.
  5. Play the tune from beginning to end, maintaining pulse even when a detail is uncertain.
  6. Learn a short passage from a recorded solo by ear. Notice how the improviser connects the harmony.
Build a living repertoire. Rather than treating repertoire as a final examination after completing theory, make it the centre of practice. Keep revisiting tunes, listening to other interpretations and playing with other musicians. Knowledge of one tune often makes the next easier to learn.

8. Listening and practice

  1. Play and sing the major ii–V–I in C, F and B♭. Listen for arrival rather than simply naming the chords.
  2. Compare each major progression with its parallel minor version using the explorer.
  3. Play only bass notes and guide tones, then add a melody of your own using just a few notes.
  4. Choose two standards from the repertoire section. Mark ii–V groups, their intended destinations and whether they resolve.
  5. Learn one of the tunes by heart, including its melody and form. Listen to at least two recordings.
  6. Transcribe a short melodic phrase across a cadence. Sing it and play it before attempting a theoretical explanation.

Use the Listening in Context resource to keep track of recordings and observations. Use Transcriptions of Jazz Solos and Jazz Melodic Dictation to develop your listening.

A note on improvisation: Recognising a ii–V–I is not the same as learning to improvise over it. The latter develops through playing repertoire, listening, transcription and musical interaction. Scales and chord symbols help describe and explore the sounds; they are not a replacement for that experience.
