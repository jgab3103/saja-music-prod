Roman Numerals and Harmonic Progressions in Bach's Chorales

Tonal relationships, harmonic analysis and composition

In Part 1, you explored Bach's chorales and their four melodic voices. In Part 2, you learnt to read figured bass and describe the intervals above a bass note. Return to Part 2.

Before we begin writing counterpoint, there is one further foundational skill to develop: Roman numeral analysis. Figured bass tells us how a chord is constructed above the bass; Roman numerals help us understand how that chord relates to a key and to the harmonies around it. Together, these two systems provide a useful language for exploring Bach's music and developing compositions of our own.

1. What is Roman numeral analysis?

Roman numeral analysis describes chords according to the scale degree of their root. It is a later analytical system, rather than the notation Bach would normally have used to teach thoroughbass. Nevertheless, it is particularly useful for studying tonal relationships in Baroque music, provided we remember that melodic movement, dissonance and local changes of key are also important.

In C major, a chord built on C is I, a chord built on D is ii, and a chord built on G is V. Uppercase numerals indicate major triads, lowercase numerals indicate minor triads, ° indicates diminished and + indicates augmented. The same relationships can be found in other keys: V is G major in C major, but A major in D major.

2. Bringing figured bass and Roman numerals together

First identify the root by rearranging the notes into thirds. Then locate that root within the key, identify the chord quality and use the bass note to determine the inversion. The figures introduced in Part 2 can be appended to the Roman numeral.

Notice that I6 and vi6 could have different roots even though both carry the figure 6. In minor, the raised leading note often creates a major V and a diminished vii°. In A minor, E–G♯–B is V, not v. A chord can also temporarily point to another tonal centre: D–F♯–A in C major may be V/V (the dominant of G), depending on what follows.

3. Harmonic movement and cadences

Roman numerals become most useful when we consider chords in succession. The tonic (I or i) provides a point of stability. Predominant harmonies such as IV and ii6 often lead towards the dominant (V or V7), which in turn creates an expectation of return to the tonic. These are tendencies, not rules that determine every chord.

ProgressionWhat to listen for
V – I / V – iDominant to tonic; the leading note tends to rise to the tonic.
ii6 – V7 – IA predominant prepares the dominant; its seventh tends to resolve down by step.
IV – V – IA clear predominant–dominant–tonic movement.
V – viA possible deceptive resolution rather than an expected tonic.
I6/4 – V – IAt a cadence the 6/4 often embellishes the dominant over a sustained dominant bass.

A cadence is a musical point of arrival, not simply any occurrence of two particular chords. Consider the rhythm, soprano, bass and phrase ending. A half cadence ends on V; an authentic cadence moves from V to I or i. A perfect authentic cadence additionally requires root-position chords and the tonic in the soprano at the arrival.

6. Composition exercises

Use manuscript paper and a piano. At this stage, concentrate on harmonic choices and the bass line rather than trying to master all the rules of four-part counterpoint.

Exercise 1 — Transpose a progression

Choose a key and a progression below. Write the Roman numerals, then write the chords on manuscript paper, using the prescribed inversion. Reveal the notes only after completing your own version.

Exercise 2 — Borrow a harmonic idea from Bach

Select a three- or four-chord occurrence from the explorer. Copy its Roman numerals and bass notes. Transpose the progression into a different key, then write a new soprano melody using chord tones. Play both versions and compare their character.

Exercise 3 — Change the bass line

Write I – IV – V – I in a key of your choice. Keep the chord roots and qualities but experiment with inversions of the first three harmonies. Keep the final tonic in root position. Write two contrasting bass lines, add a simple melody and consider which version moves more smoothly.

Exercise 4 — Compose a short phrase

Write an eight-bar phrase in a major or minor key. Aim for a half cadence around bar four and an authentic cadence at the end. Write the Roman numerals first, then a bass line and a soprano melody. If you feel confident, add alto and tenor parts to complete the harmonies. Sing each outer voice independently before playing them together.

Compare your phrase with two examples from Bach. What did you notice about the relationship between harmonic movement and the melody?
