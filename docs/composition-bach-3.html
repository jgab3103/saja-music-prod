Composition Study: Bach Chorales · Part 3

Roman Numerals in Bach's Chorales

Understanding harmonic relationships through analysis and listening

In Part 1, you explored the four voices of Bach's chorales. In Part 2, you learnt to use figured bass to describe intervals above a bass note. Before we begin exploring counterpoint, there is one further foundational skill to develop: Roman numeral analysis.

1. What are Roman numerals?

Roman numerals describe a chord in relation to a tonal centre. In C major, C–E–G is I, D–F–A is ii and G–B–D is V. In G major, G–B–D becomes I and D–F♯–A becomes V. The numeral describes the scale degree of the root, not necessarily the lowest sounding note.

Uppercase numerals indicate major triads, lowercase numerals indicate minor triads, ° indicates diminished and + indicates augmented. The figures you learnt in Part 2 can be added to indicate inversions: I6 is still the tonic chord, but with its third in the bass; V6/5 is a dominant seventh in first inversion.

In minor-key music, the sixth and seventh degrees are often altered. In A minor, raising G to G♯ creates E major (V) and G♯ diminished (vii°). Adding D to E major creates V7, whose seventh tends to resolve downwards. These are particularly useful harmonies to listen for in Bach.

Figured bass and Roman numerals answer different questions. E–G–C in C major has the figure 6 because the upper notes are a third and a sixth above E; its Roman numeral is I6 because C is the root. The same figure over C could describe A minor in first inversion (vi6). Always distinguish the bass from the root.

2. What Roman numerals can and cannot tell us

Roman numeral analysis is a later analytical convention, not the system Bach normally used when teaching thoroughbass. It is a useful way of understanding harmonic relationships, but it cannot fully describe the melodic activity taking place in four independent voices. Passing notes, suspensions and other dissonances do not always require a new Roman numeral.

There is also the question of modulation. In C major, D–F♯–A–C may be V7/V (the dominant seventh of G). If the music goes on to establish G major, however, that same chord may be understood as V7 in the new key. Listen to the whole phrase and its cadence before deciding. A brief tonicisation is not necessarily a modulation.

As discussed in Part 1, some Baroque key signatures differ from modern conventions. The written signature is a clue, not proof of the tonal centre. Listen for points of rest and examine the bass and the leading note.

3. Three examples from Bach

Study these short passages. The Roman numerals are selected, human-written analytical annotations, not a label for every sounding combination of notes. Some beats have deliberately been left unlabelled; the notes in between are an invitation to investigate. Listen to the original voicing and follow the relationship between tonic and dominant.

Jesu, der du meine Seele · BWV 354

Measures 1–2 · B♭ tonal centre · four-flat (Dorian) signature

The signature contains B♭, E♭, A♭ and D♭. Although a modern B♭ minor signature would also contain G♭, this older Dorian signature is compatible with a B♭-centred minor-tonal reading. The tonic (i) is B♭–D♭–F; the dominant (V) is F–A–C.

Notice the immediate movement between i and its first inversion (i6), then V and back to i. In measure 2, G♭ in the bass supports iv6 (E♭ minor in first inversion), before the dominant returns. A natural supplies the leading note, and E♭ briefly supplies the seventh of V7. The phrase comes to rest on B♭ minor.

Listen to the eighth notes between these harmonies. Which appear to connect one chord to the next?

Kommt her zu mir, spricht Gottes Sohn · BWV 74/8

Measures 3–4 · A minor · no sharps or flats

There are no sharps or flats in the signature, but the tonal centre of this passage is A, not C. The tonic (i) is A–C–E, and the major dominant (V) is E–G♯–B. G♯, the raised seventh degree, gives the dominant its strong tendency towards A.

Measure 3 moves i–V–i before opening out to III (C major). In measure 4, the diminished seventh chord G♯–B–D–F (vii°7) leads back to i. Listen for the shared dominant tendency of V and vii°7. The fermata on E major marks a half cadence; the following i begins the next phrase.

Can you hear the contrast between resting on the dominant and resting on the tonic?

Christ lag in Todesbanden · BWV 278

Measures 3–4 · E minor · one sharp (F♯)

One sharp might suggest G major or E minor. Here the tonic is E–G–B (i), while the dominant is B–D♯–F♯ (V), with D♯ functioning as the leading note.

In measure 3, iiø7 and V4/3 prepare i6; a brief vii°6 also points towards E. Notice how the tonic and dominant families can be elaborated through inversions and seventh chords without losing the sense of tonal direction.

At the start of measure 4, E sounds above a B bass before D♯ appears: a 4–3 motion over the dominant. The bass remains on B through the following V and V7, before the voices settle on E minor at the fermata. The Roman numeral V describes the underlying harmonic function, even as individual notes move.

5. Analysis and composition exercises

Download some manuscript paper or print a chorale using the explorer. Begin with one or two phrases rather than trying to analyse a whole chorale immediately.

  1. Write out the key signature and identify the tonal centre. Write the tonic and dominant triads, their inversions and the dominant seventh in that key.
  2. Copy a short passage onto a grand staff. Write figured bass beneath the bass line, then add Roman numerals below it. Work from the bass and the sounding notes, and distinguish the chord root from the lowest note.
  3. Circle the points at which the music appears to come to rest. Look for the relationships between I/i, V, V7 and vii°. If a passage suggests a new tonal centre, write your proposed key above the staff and explain why. Leave a question mark where you are uncertain.
  4. Choose a short harmonic progression from your analysis and transpose it to another key. Write a new bass line and soprano using the same harmonic relationships. Play or sing the result and compare it with Bach's original.

There is not always a single convincing interpretation. Keep your written work, listen carefully and discuss alternative readings with your teacher or other musicians.
