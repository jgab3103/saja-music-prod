Theory Fundamentals · Part 1

The Twelve Notes & the Major Scale

Music theory · Beginner · Interactive lesson

There are twelve notes in an octave. By organising those notes into patterns of tones and semitones, we can construct scales. Our first example is the major scale.

Before you begin: learn the piano keys and the treble clef

Learning where notes are on the piano keyboard is essential. It gives you a simple, visible map of the relationships between notes. Even if you play another instrument, this will make music theory much easier.

Start learning to read notes in the treble clef too. You do not need to know them all yet. We will introduce staff notation gradually in a later lesson; for now, we will use the piano keyboard to see and hear everything.

1. There are twelve notes in an octave

There are twelve different notes in an octave in the equal-tempered system used by modern pianos. Start at C and move upwards, playing every key, black and white. After twelve steps you arrive at the next C. The note name repeats, but the pitch is higher.

The distance from one C to the next C is called an octave. Each step to the very next piano key is a semitone.

Explore the twelve notes

Play every key from the first C to the next C, including all the black keys. Count twelve steps. Then keep playing to the next C. Notice that exactly the same pattern of twelve notes repeats in the next octave.

Click or tap a key to hear it. Try playing C4, C5 and C6: they have the same note name, but each sounds an octave higher.

Choose a key to hear its sound and see its name.

Moving through all twelve notes in order creates the chromatic scale:

CC♯ / D♭DD♯ / E♭EFF♯ / G♭GG♯ / A♭AA♯ / B♭BC

C♯ and D♭ are different names for the same piano key. These are enharmonic equivalents. We will learn why we choose one spelling over another when we construct scales.

2. We use words to describe the distance between notes

Notes can be close together or far apart. We use special terms to describe the distance between two notes. This distance is called an interval.

Two keys directly beside each other are one semitone apart. Look at the keyboard: moving from one key to the very next key, whether white or black, is a semitone. C to C♯ is a semitone. E to F is also a semitone because those white keys are immediately next to one another.

Two keys with exactly one key between them are one tone apart. Move two steps along the keyboard. C to D is one tone because C♯ is between them. E to F♯ is also one tone because F is between them.

So a tone is made of two semitones. We will use these two distances to build scales.

A semitone: C to C♯
CC♯

Neighbouring keys: one semitone.

A tone: C to D
CC♯D

Two steps: one tone.

Remember: E–F and B–C are semitones too, despite being white keys next to each other.

3. A pattern of tones and semitones creates a scale

A scale is a group of notes organised in a particular order. To build one, we choose a starting note and follow a pattern of distances. Some steps are tones (two keys); others are semitones (one key). Change the pattern, and you change the kind of scale.

For example, start on C. Move up a tone (two keys) to D, another tone to E, then just one semitone to F. Keep following the remaining steps until you reach C again. The notes you have found make the C major scale.

Every major scale uses exactly the same sequence of steps:

TTSTTTS

T = tone (2 semitones). S = semitone (1 semitone). The seven steps add up to 12 semitones: one octave.

C major, one step at a time
CDEFGABC

The two semitones are between E–F (notes 3–4) and B–C (notes 7–8).

The pattern determines the kind of scale; the starting note determines its name. Start on G and use the same pattern, and you get G major. Start on B♭, and you get B♭ major. The note names change, but the sequence of intervals does not.

5. Make a melody from the major scale

A scale gives us a collection of notes. We do not have to play them in order. We can choose notes from that scale to create a melody.
