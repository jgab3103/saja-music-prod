C Major — Across the Guitar Fretboard

Guitar · Major scale and modes · Fretboard positions · Interactive tablature

Learn where the seven notes of C major (C, D, E, F, G, A and B) sit on the guitar. Explore all seven compact positions together, then practise the same notes as the seven modes of the major scale and in sequences of thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths and sevenths.

1. Getting started

All seven modes in this study use the notes C–D–E–F–G–A–B. Changing the mode changes the tonal centre and accompanying harmony, not the available notes on the guitar. For an introduction to modes, their history and their use in improvisation, see Understanding the Modes of the Major Scale.

Explore the seven closed positions below. Each spans four semitones at most, uses no open strings and retains the lower-fret fingering when the same sounding pitch occurs twice. Aim to move freely between positions rather than memorising isolated shapes.

4. Practise in intervals

First, play the notes in the selected position up and down. Then choose an interval below. The tablature shows every ascending and descending pair available in the position, beginning on the lowest note of the lower strings. Scroll horizontally to follow the complete exercise, all the way up and back down in one continuous tab. An interval of a third means playing one scale degree and then the note two degrees above it; fourths, fifths, sixths and sevenths follow the same principle.

These are diatonic intervals, so their semitone sizes vary according to the starting note. The tablature is a playable ascending and descending example, not a prescribed fingering. Changing mode only changes the root highlights; the interval pattern and tablature do not change. Listen for the new centre over an appropriate drone or harmony.

5. Improvise with a play-along

Select a mode above, then start the accompaniment and improvise using any of the seven C-major positions. The fretboard notes remain exactly the same; the bass and chord change to establish the mode’s own tonal centre. Listen for the distinctive character of each mode rather than simply beginning the scale on a different note.

The accompaniment stays on one home chord, with a soft bass pulse and slowly shifting, spacious voicings. Some bars add a seventh or ninth (and occasionally a characteristic modal colour). There are no chord changes to chase: you can focus on hearing the mode.

6. Developing your practice

Learn the names of the notes before worrying about speed. Identify each note on every string within the position, then play slowly in both directions while saying or singing its name. Practise thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths and sevenths, first ascending and then descending. Move into the next position without using open strings. Because the exercise begins from the lowest note of each closed position, the pattern is easy to compare across the neck.

Finally, explore each mode against a sustained note or chord that establishes its tonal centre — for example, D for Dorian or F for Lydian. This helps make the character of the mode audible, rather than simply playing C major from a different starting note.
