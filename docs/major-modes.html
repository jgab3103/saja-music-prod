Understanding the Modes of the Major Scale

Harmony and Counterpoint · Scales · Modal history · Improvisation

1. What is a scale?

In Western music, we generally work with twelve different pitches within an octave. A scale is a selection of notes from these twelve pitches, organised in relation to a central note known as the tonic.

Different selections of notes create different musical characters. A scale is therefore much more than a sequence of notes to practise on an instrument. It provides a collection of musical materials from which melodies, harmonies and improvisations can be constructed.

One of the most familiar examples is the major scale. C major is made up of seven notes: C–D–E–F–G–A–B. Its interval pattern is whole–whole–half–whole–whole–whole–half.

Students unfamiliar with the construction of major scales should first explore Theory Fundamentals – The Major Scale.

To explore how different selections of notes create different musical characters, visit the Sound of Scales Lab. This resource allows students to listen to melodies constructed from different scales against a consistent tonal centre.

2. What is a mode?

One of the interesting things about a collection of notes is that its musical character depends on which note is experienced as the tonic — the note of rest, resolution, or musical "home".

Consider the seven notes of C major:

C–D–E–F–G–A–B

When C is established as the tonal centre, the relationships between C and the remaining notes give us the familiar sound of the C major scale, also known as C Ionian.

However, if D is established as the tonal centre, the same collection of notes produces a different musical character:

D–E–F–G–A–B–C

This is known as D Dorian. Although it contains exactly the same notes as C major, the relationships between the tonic and the other notes have changed. D now has a minor third above it (F), a perfect fifth (A), a major sixth (B) and a minor seventh (C).

Similarly, if A is established as the tonal centre, the same collection of notes produces A Aeolian, otherwise known as the A natural minor scale:

A–B–C–D–E–F–G

Importantly, playing a scale from a different starting note does not, by itself, establish a mode. The musical accompaniment, bass notes, melodic emphasis and points of resolution all contribute to establishing which note is experienced as the tonal centre.

The seven modes derived from the notes of C major are:

C Ionian: C–D–E–F–G–A–B
D Dorian: D–E–F–G–A–B–C
E Phrygian: E–F–G–A–B–C–D
F Lydian: F–G–A–B–C–D–E
G Mixolydian: G–A–B–C–D–E–F
A Aeolian: A–B–C–D–E–F–G
B Locrian: B–C–D–E–F–G–A

These are often referred to as relative modes because they share the same collection of notes but have different tonal centres.

Try listening: Play the same white notes on a piano over a sustained C, then over a sustained D. Notice how the sense of home changes. Explore the Sound of Scales Lab.

3. A brief history of modes

Ancient Greek terminology

Although the names of the modes have origins in ancient Greek musical theory, their meanings have changed considerably throughout history. The modes used by contemporary musicians are not identical to those used in ancient Greek or Medieval music.

Medieval and Renaissance

Modes have a long history in Western music, predating the widespread adoption of the major and minor tonal system. During the Medieval and Renaissance periods, modal systems provided an important framework for organising melodies and composing music, particularly in the traditions of Gregorian chant and sacred polyphony.

17th–18th centuries

During the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the major and minor tonal system gradually became the dominant framework for Western classical composition. Nevertheless, traces of earlier modal practices remained.

Bach and the Baroque

Even in the music of J. S. Bach, we encounter older modal traditions and conventions. His Toccata and Fugue in D minor (BWV 538), for example, is commonly known as the "Dorian" Toccata and Fugue because of its use of a Dorian-style key signature, although the work itself largely operates within the tonal framework of D minor.

20th century and modal jazz

In the twentieth century, modes became particularly important again in jazz, alongside their extensive use in folk music, rock, film music and contemporary composition. The development of modal jazz, particularly through recordings such as Miles Davis's Kind of Blue (1959), demonstrated how musicians could create rich and sophisticated improvisations without relying on rapidly changing chord progressions.

Contemporary practice

In contemporary improvisation, it is often useful to think of a scale or mode simply as a selection of notes from the twelve available pitches, organised around a tonal centre and providing a particular musical character.

4. Understanding the character of each mode

While deriving the seven modes from a single major scale is a useful way of understanding their construction, it is not necessarily the most useful way of understanding their individual sounds.

For improvisation, it is often more helpful to compare different modes using the same tonic. These are known as parallel modes.

Dorian and natural minor (Aeolian)

Compare the following two scales:

D Aeolian: D–E–F–G–A–B♭–C
D Dorian: D–E–F–G–A–B–C

Both scales contain a minor third, a perfect fifth and a minor seventh. The only difference is the sixth degree of the scale. Dorian has a natural sixth, whereas Aeolian has a flattened sixth.

This seemingly small difference can have a significant effect on the character of a melody. The natural sixth of Dorian can provide a more open or lifted quality, while the flattened sixth of Aeolian can produce a darker or more sombre character.

Both scales can be used to construct melodies over a D minor chord, but each offers a different musical colour.

Ionian and Lydian

C Ionian: C–D–E–F–G–A–B
C Lydian: C–D–E–F♯–G–A–B

Lydian differs from the ordinary major scale by having a raised fourth degree. In this example, F becomes F sharp.

Both scales have a major third and a major seventh, but the raised fourth of Lydian creates a distinctive colour that is often experienced as spacious, floating or expansive. This sound is particularly common in film music, contemporary jazz and ambient music.

Ionian and Mixolydian

C Ionian: C–D–E–F–G–A–B
C Mixolydian: C–D–E–F–G–A–B♭

Mixolydian differs from the major scale by having a flattened seventh degree. It retains the major third but does not have the strong leading note of the ordinary major scale.

This gives Mixolydian a different sense of movement and resolution. It is particularly common in blues, rock, folk music, funk and jazz.

Phrygian and natural minor

E Aeolian: E–F♯–G–A–B–C–D
E Phrygian: E–F–G–A–B–C–D

Phrygian differs from the natural minor scale by having a flattened second degree. The close proximity of this note to the tonic creates a distinctive tension, providing another melodic colour that can be explored over minor harmony.

Locrian

B Locrian: B–C–D–E–F–G–A

Locrian is distinctive because it contains both a flattened second and a flattened fifth. Unlike the other six modes, its tonic triad is diminished rather than major or minor. This makes it considerably more challenging to establish as a stable tonal centre, but it offers interesting possibilities for improvisation over diminished and half-diminished harmony.

Descriptions such as bright, dark, spacious or tense are not fixed emotional properties of a scale. They are useful starting points for listening. Rhythm, articulation, tempo, harmony and musical context also contribute enormously to how a scale is experienced.

5. The interval structure of the seven modes

Each mode can be described in terms of its intervals relative to the major scale built on the same tonic. The following table provides a useful reference for understanding what gives each mode its distinctive musical character.

Mode Interval structure Distinguishing features
Ionian 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 Major scale
Dorian 1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7 Minor with a natural sixth
Phrygian 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 Minor with a flattened second
Lydian 1 2 3 ♯4 5 6 7 Major with a raised fourth
Mixolydian 1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7 Major with a flattened seventh
Aeolian 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 Natural minor scale
Locrian 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭7 Minor with a flattened second and fifth

Rather than simply memorising these interval structures, students should spend time listening to the characteristic notes of each mode and discovering how they influence the sound of a melody.

6. Modes as a vehicle for improvisation

One of the most effective ways to begin improvising is to establish a single chord as the harmonic centre of the music and explore the melodic possibilities of a particular scale or mode over that chord.

Unlike music that moves through a series of chord progressions, this approach allows the improviser to remain within a consistent harmonic environment. The underlying harmony may remain unchanged for many bars, or even for an entire piece of music.

In this context, we can think of the underlying chord as the tonic chord — the chord built on the first degree of the mode. It provides a harmonic point of rest around which the improvisation develops.

The tonic chord does not necessarily have to be major. Depending on the mode, it may be major, minor, dominant seventh or diminished in quality.

Mode Tonic triad Tonic seventh chord
C Ionian C major Cmaj7
D Dorian D minor Dm7
E Phrygian E minor Em7
F Lydian F major Fmaj7
G Mixolydian G major G7
A Aeolian A minor Am7
B Locrian B diminished Bm7♭5

Notice that all seven chords are constructed from the same seven notes, yet each can serve as the harmonic centre of a different modal environment, although Locrian is particularly unstable.

Consider G Mixolydian, for example. Its tonic seventh chord is G7. In conventional tonal harmony, G7 would frequently function as the dominant chord of C major and create an expectation of resolution to C.

In modal improvisation, however, G7 can itself be the point of rest. There is no requirement for it to resolve to C major. Although it has the structure of a dominant seventh chord, it is functioning as the tonic of the modal environment.

This distinction between chord quality and harmonic function is an important one. A dominant seventh chord does not always have to function as a dominant, just as a minor chord does not necessarily have to function as the second or sixth chord of a major key.

In each of these examples, the underlying chord is built on scale degree one. In Roman numeral analysis, we distinguish their qualities using symbols such as Imaj7, i7, I7 and iø7, rather than labelling them all with an uppercase I.

7. Choosing a mode to play over a chord

In contemporary jazz and other improvisational traditions, musicians often approach scales and modes from the perspective of the underlying chord.

Rather than asking which major scale a particular mode belongs to, the improviser might ask: What collection of notes can I use over this chord, and what musical character will those notes create?

A single chord can often accommodate several different scales or modes. Each contains the basic notes of the chord but offers different additional melodic possibilities.

Improvising over a major seventh chord

Consider a Cmaj7 chord, which contains the notes:

C–E–G–B

Both C Ionian and C Lydian contain all four of these notes:

  • C Ionian: C–D–E–F–G–A–B
  • C Lydian: C–D–E–F♯–G–A–B

Both modes are compatible with the basic structure of the chord. However, the natural fourth in Ionian and the raised fourth in Lydian provide different melodic possibilities.

When sustained against the E of the Cmaj7 chord, the natural fourth (F) can create a pronounced dissonance. The raised fourth of Lydian (F sharp), often described harmonically as a sharp eleventh, provides a different kind of tension and is frequently used as a colour in contemporary jazz harmony.

Improvising over a minor seventh chord

Consider a Dm7 chord, which contains the notes:

D–F–A–C

There are three modes derived from the major scale that contain all four of these chord tones:

  • D Dorian: D–E–F–G–A–B–C
  • D Aeolian: D–E–F–G–A–B♭–C
  • D Phrygian: D–E♭–F–G–A–B♭–C

All three modes share the tonic, minor third, perfect fifth and minor seventh of the underlying chord. Their differences lie in the remaining notes available to the improviser.

Dorian offers a natural sixth, Aeolian offers a flattened sixth, and Phrygian offers both a flattened second and a flattened sixth.

Imagine improvising over a sustained Dm7 chord for several minutes. By moving between these three collections of notes, the improviser can explore quite different musical environments without changing the underlying chord at all.

Improvising over a dominant seventh chord

Consider a G7 chord:

G–B–D–F

G Mixolydian contains all four chord tones and provides a useful starting point for improvisation:

G–A–B–C–D–E–F

More advanced approaches to improvisation may also draw on other scales, including Lydian dominant, the altered scale, diminished scales and whole-tone scales. Each provides different tensions and melodic possibilities.

Improvising over a half-diminished chord

Consider a Bm7♭5 chord:

B–D–F–A

B Locrian contains all four of these notes:

B–C–D–E–F–G–A

Locrian therefore provides a useful starting point for exploring half-diminished harmony. A fully diminished seventh chord has a different interval structure and requires other considerations.

8. Chord tones and melodic colour

One useful way of thinking about improvisation is to distinguish between the notes that form the underlying chord and the additional notes that provide melodic colour.

For example, when improvising using D Dorian over Dm7:

  • Chord tones: D, F, A, C
  • Additional scale tones: E, G, B

The chord tones establish a clear relationship with the underlying harmony. The remaining notes provide additional possibilities for movement, tension, colour and expression.

If the improviser changes from D Dorian to D Aeolian, the underlying chord tones remain exactly the same. Only one of the additional scale tones changes: B becomes B flat.

This is one of the most interesting aspects of modal improvisation. A musician can change the character of the music considerably by altering just one or two notes in the available collection.

Of course, not every note in a compatible scale will sound equally settled at every moment. The voicing of the accompanying chord, any extensions it contains, the surrounding harmony and the way notes are approached and resolved all influence their effectiveness.

These relationships should be understood as possibilities for musical exploration, rather than rigid rules about which notes are permitted over particular chords.
