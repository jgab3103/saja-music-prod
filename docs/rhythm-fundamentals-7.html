Rhythm Fundamentals · Part 7

Reading Rhythm Notation

We have been feeling the beat, dividing it into different numbers of notes, and speaking rhythms using ta. Now let's see how musicians write those rhythms down.

Beams help us see the beat

A quarter note can fill one beat on its own. Two eighth notes can share that beat, and their stems are often joined by a beam. Four sixteenth notes can also share a beat, usually joined by two beams. Beams make groups easier to recognise at a glance.

1 note in a beat

One quarter note. One ta.

2 notes in a beat

Two eighth notes, joined by one beam. Ta-ta.

4 notes in a beat

Four sixteenth notes, joined by two beams. Ta-ta-ta-ta.

Other divisions

Three, five, six or seven evenly spaced notes can also fit inside one beat.

What does a tuplet look like?

When we divide a beat into an unusual number of equal parts, we use a tuplet. The notes are grouped together, with a small number above them. A 3 marks a triplet; 5 a quintuplet; 6 a sextuplet; and 7 a septuplet. The notes still fit into the same single beat.

You'll see those numbers and beams in the exercise below. There is no need to memorise everything at once: start with 1, 2, 3 and 4 notes per beat, then add the others when you're ready.

Endless exercise — read, feel and say

Keep a steady four-beat pulse with your foot. Say “ta” whenever a note begins. Follow the notation as it plays. The beat stays steady even when the notes within it become faster or slower.

Notes in each beat (choose at least one)

Two bars of 4/4. New rhythms appear as you play.

Each bar has four quarter-note beats. In grand-staff view, the treble and bass staves share the same rhythm; they are not separate exercises. Sound is a simple click, not a pitched melody.
