Building and Naming Jazz Chords

Jazz Harmony 1 · Theory, Harmony & Counterpoint · Chord construction · Voicing

1. From scales to harmony

In the previous five Jazz Theory lessons, we explored pitch collections as sources of melodic and harmonic colour. We now turn our attention to the sounds produced when notes are combined. Jazz harmony is not simply a collection of more complicated chords: it is a way of organising sonorities, creating movement between voices and shaping musical expression.

In this lesson we will concentrate on building and naming individual chords. In subsequent lessons we will explore how chords relate to one another, common progressions, and how these ideas help us understand the harmony of jazz standards.

Before beginning: Students who are unfamiliar with intervals, triads, inversions or seventh chords should revisit the Theory Fundamentals learning pathway. The following lessons are especially relevant: Theory Fundamentals 1 (major scales), Theory Fundamentals 3 (intervals), Theory Fundamentals 6 (triads), Theory Fundamentals 7 (inversions) and Theory Fundamentals 8 (seventh chords).

2. Building chords in thirds

Much of the vocabulary of jazz harmony has its origins in tertian harmony: chords constructed by stacking major and minor thirds. Begin with C major (C–D–E–F–G–A–B). Selecting every second scale degree gives C–E–G–B–D–F–A. The first three notes form a triad; adding the fourth produces a seventh chord. Continuing beyond the octave produces extensions.

From a triad to a seventh chord

C–E–G → C–E–G–B

C major becomes Cmaj7. The third E establishes major quality; B gives the major seventh colour.

Changing the quality

C–E♭–G → C–E♭–G–B♭

Lowering the third produces C minor. Adding a minor seventh produces Cm7. Notice that the interval between adjacent chord tones changes.

TriadNotesFormulaStacked thirds
C majorC E G1 3 5Major, minor
C minorC E♭ G1 ♭3 5Minor, major
C diminishedC E♭ G♭1 ♭3 ♭5Minor, minor
C augmentedC E G♯1 3 ♯5Major, major

All interval formulas in this lesson are measured against the major scale on the chord root. They describe distances from that root, not the intervals between adjacent notes. A chord does not need to be drawn from a single major scale.

3. Seventh chords: the essential vocabulary

The third and seventh are particularly important in jazz. Together they distinguish several chord qualities even when the fifth is omitted. These notes are often called guide tones, and their movement becomes especially important when we study progressions.

B double flat sounds like A in equal temperament, but its spelling shows that it is a diminished seventh above C, not a sixth. C6 (C–E–G–A) is a different chord from Cdim7.

Quality is not function: C7 has a dominant seventh structure, but it need not function as the dominant of F. It might be the tonic of a blues or a sustained modal harmony. We will distinguish chord symbols from harmonic function in later lessons.

4. Extensions: ninths, elevenths and thirteenths

Continue stacking thirds above the seventh. A ninth is a second displaced by an octave; an eleventh is a fourth; a thirteenth is a sixth. These names describe their place in the chord structure, not necessarily the register in which they must be played.

DegreeNote above CSemitones from CExample
9D14C9
11F17C11
13A21C13

Major extensions

Cmaj9 = C E G B D

Cmaj13 can include A above the major seventh B. The natural eleventh F is often omitted because of its tension against E.

Minor extensions

Cm9 = C E♭ G B♭ D

Cm11 adds F. Cm13 makes A available as a natural thirteenth, a colour associated with Dorian.

Dominant extensions

C9 = C E G B♭ D

C13 adds A to the dominant sound. A typical practical voicing is C–E–B♭–D–A; the fifth and eleventh are omitted.

Added notes are different

Cadd9 = C E G D

Cadd9 does not imply a seventh. C6 = C E G A; C6/9 = C E G A D. C9, by contrast, implies a minor seventh.

A written extension does not mean every intervening note must be sounded. In particular, an unaltered eleventh over a major third can be very dissonant; jazz musicians frequently omit it, treat it melodically, or use a suspended voicing. The chord symbol describes a harmonic intention rather than a fixed inventory of simultaneous notes.

5. Alterations and suspended chords

Extensions and chord tones may be raised or lowered by a semitone. In jazz, altered dominant chords are particularly common, although alterations also occur in other chord qualities.

SymbolMeaningExample notes

C7♯5Dominant seventh with raised fifthC E G♯ B♭
C7♭13Dominant seventh with lowered thirteenthC E G B♭ A♭
C7altAltered dominant; selection of altered ninths/fifthsC E B♭ D♭ A♭ (one possibility)
C7sus4Fourth replaces third in basic suspended voicingC F G B♭
Csus2Second replaces thirdC D G

Although G♯ and A♭ sound the same in equal temperament, ♯5 and ♭13 describe different harmonic relationships. A ♭13 may coexist with a natural fifth, whereas a ♯5 changes the fifth itself. Likewise ♯11 need not replace the fifth, while ♭5 does.

A suspended chord is not inherently major or minor because the third is replaced. In jazz it may remain suspended without resolving; some extended sus voicings even include the third in a separate register.

Connecting with the earlier lessons: Compare C7(♯11) with C Lydian dominant, C7(♭9,♭13) with C Phrygian dominant, and C7alt with C altered. Revisit Harmonic Minor Modes, Melodic Minor Modes and Symmetrical Scales to hear these colours. A scale offers possibilities; it does not prescribe a voicing.

6. How to read and name a jazz chord

Read a chord symbol in layers: identify its root, basic quality, seventh, extensions or alterations, and any specified bass note. Chord notation varies between publishers and musicians, so recognise equivalent forms rather than assuming a universal system.

SymbolRoot and qualityAdditional information
F♯m9F♯ minorMinor seventh and natural ninth
B♭maj7(♯11)B♭ major seventhRaised eleventh
E7(♭9,♯5)E dominant seventhLowered ninth and raised fifth
Dm7♭5D minor seventhLowered fifth
G13sus4G suspended dominantMinor seventh and thirteenth; fourth in place of third
Cmaj7/EC major seventhE must be the bass note
D/CD major triadC must be the bass note

In a slash chord the note after the slash specifies the bass; it may be a member of the chord (an inversion) or a note outside it. D/C, for example, gives C in the bass beneath D–F♯–A. It can also be heard as a partial C dominant sonority with 9, ♯11 and 13, but the two symbols do not communicate exactly the same information.

When naming an unfamiliar collection of notes, first look for possible roots and identify the intervals above each. Consider the bass, the preceding and following chords, and the intended musical function. The lowest note is not always the root, and the same pitches can have more than one useful name.

7. Voicing chords: from thirds to fourths

A chord describes harmonic identity, while a voicing describes how its notes are distributed between registers and instruments. Jazz voicings frequently depart from the close-position stack of thirds used to explain chord construction. A pianist or guitarist may omit notes, double notes, spread them across octaves, or organise them into fourths.

Close and open voicings

Cmaj7: C4–E4–G4–B4

Spread the same notes: C3–G3–B3–E4. The chord identity remains Cmaj7, but the spacing and character change.

Shell and rootless voicings

C7 shell: C3–E3–B♭3

With a bassist playing C, a pianist might play E3–B♭3–D4–A4, a rootless C13 voicing. The fifth and root can be absent from the upper instrument without losing the harmonic context.

Quartal harmony

D–G–C–F

Three consecutive perfect fourths produce a sonority that can be heard over D as a partial Dm11 chord (1, 11, ♭7, ♭3). The absence of a fifth and ninth is not a problem.

Quartal voicings and modal jazz

E–A–D–G–B

Fourths topped by a major third evoke the voicing language associated with McCoy Tyner and modal jazz. Over E bass this can suggest Em11; over C bass it can suggest Cmaj13 with a natural thirteenth (A) and major seventh (B). Context changes the interpretation.

Quartal harmony is not necessarily a completely separate system of chord naming. Sometimes a conventional chord symbol describes the underlying harmony while the player chooses a fourth-based voicing. In other contexts, a stack of fourths may be intended as an independent sonority with no clear tertian root. Writing the actual notes or specifying a voicing may communicate more than forcing an elaborate chord symbol onto it.

Listen to the opening chordal material of Miles Davis’s So What (1959), associated with Bill Evans’s voicings, and to McCoy Tyner’s accompaniment on John Coltrane’s modal recordings. Notice the space, register and movement of the voices rather than merely naming each chord.

8. Chord construction reference and practice

Use the following interactive reference to build chords on different roots. The notes are spelled according to their interval roles, and the audio lets students compare a stacked chord with its arpeggiated sound. These are illustrative voicings, not compulsory performance arrangements.

Listening and construction exercises

  1. Write major, minor, diminished and augmented triads on C, F and B♭. Add sevenths to construct the basic jazz chord qualities.
  2. Compare Cmaj7, C7 and Cm7. Identify the changing third and seventh by ear.
  3. Compare Cadd9, C9 and Cmaj9. Explain why the three symbols are not interchangeable.
  4. Build C7(♭9), C7(♯9), C7(♯11) and C13. Listen to the colour of each extension rather than just naming it.
  5. Play D–F–A–C, then D–G–C–F. Compare the tertian and quartal sounds over D bass.
  6. Play Cmaj9 in different registers and inversions. Omit the fifth and listen to what remains. Try keeping a C bass while changing the upper voices.
  7. Choose a chord symbol from a jazz lead sheet and construct two different voicings. Do not worry yet about analysing the whole progression.
What comes next? Being able to construct and name chords is the beginning of harmonic understanding, not its end. We will next explore diatonic seventh chords, guide-tone movement, the major and minor ii–V–I, turnarounds and other common progressions. These ideas will then provide a framework for analysing the harmony of jazz standards. As with the scale lessons, the aim is to hear and understand musical relationships, not to calculate chord symbols at extraordinary speed.
