7. Why do we use scales? Create a melody

Scales give us a collection of notes to work with. By choosing a smaller group from the twelve possible notes in an octave, we can begin to make interesting melodies. A scale does not tell us which notes to play, in what order, or for how long. Those choices are where the composing begins.

Listen to the way composers shape melodies. Mozart’s Eine kleine Nachtmusik begins in G major; the Minuet in G major in the Anna Magdalena Bach notebook (composed by Christian Petzold) is another familiar melody to explore. They use notes from a key in recognisable patterns, but it is the movement, repetition and rhythm that bring those notes to life. Music can also use notes outside the scale — for now, we are starting with a deliberately limited collection.

Try composing with just one scale. Begin on degree 1, move by step or leap to other degrees, repeat a note if you like, and find a way back to 1. Listen to what happens when you change only one or two notes. You do not need harmony to make a satisfying melody.

8. Put it on paper

Writing music by hand is an excellent way to check that you really understand the patterns. Print some blank manuscript paper and write out the major, natural minor, harmonic minor and ascending melodic minor scales you have learned. Then practise writing key signatures, beginning with C major and adding one sharp or flat at a time.

Written revision exercise

Write scales and key signatures

Choose several starting notes. Write each scale on the treble staff, using the correct note names and accidentals. Then write the major key signatures in sharp order and flat order. Check your work against the scale explorer and key signature exercises above. Finally, write out one or two melodies you composed in Section 7 — notate each note on the treble staff, and try changing the order of the notes to create a new musical phrase.
