Theory Fundamentals · Part 5

Playing Notes Together: Intervals, Chords & Triads

Listening · Building chords · Major and minor scales · Treble-clef notation

Until now, we have mostly played or written notes one after another. But music can also combine notes at the same time. Two notes sound an interval. Three or more notes together can create a chord. Let's listen before we start giving chords names.

1. Two notes sounding together

We learned that intervals describe the distance between two notes. Now we can hear that distance. A minor second is one semitone, a major second is two, a minor third is three and a major third is four. Each creates a different sound when the notes play together.

2. Stack intervals to make your own chords

We do not have to stop at two notes. We can put another note above the last one, then another. The distances between successive notes can be different. For example, try a minor third, followed by a major second, followed by a perfect fourth.

There are many possible chords. Some are built from thirds, while others use seconds, fourths or more unusual combinations. Here, the goal is simply to experiment and listen.

Chord stacking laboratory

Choose a distance to add a new note above the highest one. Up to five notes can sound together.

3. Why are thirds so important?

Return to a scale. We can number its notes 1–2–3–4–5–6–7. If we begin on note 1, skip note 2, play note 3, skip note 4, and play note 5, we get 1–3–5. Those notes form a triad — a three-note chord built by stacking thirds.

Much tonal music uses chords built from thirds. The particular distances between the bottom, middle and top notes determine the type of triad. We'll study chord progressions later; for now, we are learning what the individual chords are made of.

4. Four types of triad

There are four fundamental triad qualities. Each uses a third above the root and a fifth above the root. The semitone distances between the three notes determine the quality.

Minor3 + 4 semitones

5. Build a triad yourself

A triad starts with a root, a third and a fifth. Choose the third and fifth yourself, then check whether you made the requested major, minor, diminished or augmented triad. Notice that a diminished fifth is six semitones above the root, and an augmented fifth is eight.

6. Build triads from a scale

Go back to our scales. Starting on each degree, take every second scale note: for example, 1–3–5, then 2–4–6, then 3–5–7. Each starting degree gives a different triad, even though all three notes come from the same scale.
