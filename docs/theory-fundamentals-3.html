Theory Fundamentals · Part 3

Intervals & the Treble Clef

From the piano keyboard to written music · Interactive lesson

You already know that a scale is a pattern of tones and semitones. Now we are going to give the distances between notes names, and learn how to see those notes written on a musical staff.

Keep practising! The piano keyboard and written notation describe the same sounds. Spend a little time with Find That Piano Key! and Find That Note! as you work through this lesson. Here we will concentrate on the treble clef. The other clefs can come later.

1. Notes have distances between them

When two notes sit on neighbouring piano keys, they are one semitone apart. When there is exactly one key between them, they are one tone (two semitones) apart.

But we use another set of names for the distance between notes. These are called intervals. We count intervals using the letter names, including both the first note and the last note.

C to D

Second: count C(1), D(2).

It is a major second: two semitones.

C to E

Third: count C(1), D(2), E(3).

It is a major third: four semitones.

Explore the distance on the piano

Choose two notes on the keyboard. Listen to each note, then see how many semitones lie between them. The keys show both names of each black note.

Select your first note.

Counting keys gives the distance in semitones. To name an interval fully, you also need the spelled note names. C–D♯ and C–E♭ sound the same on a piano, but are written as different intervals.

2. Introducing the treble clef

Music is written on a staff: five horizontal lines with four spaces between them. The treble clef tells us which notes the lines and spaces represent. The clef curls around the second line from the bottom: G.

Notes climb as we move upward through lines and spaces. On the treble staff, the lines from bottom to top are E–G–B–D–F. The spaces are F–A–C–E.

The five lines: E – G – B – D – F
One note on each line, from the lowest line to the highest.
The four spaces: F – A – C – E
One note in each space, from the lowest to the highest.

Some notes fall above or below the five lines. We add little ledger lines to show them. Middle C (C4) is just below the treble staff on a ledger line. As you go higher, the notes on the page move up too.

From middle C upwards
C4 begins on a ledger line below the staff. Notice the notes alternate between spaces and lines.

3. Intervals have numbers and qualities

An interval's number tells you how many letter names it spans. Its quality tells you the precise distance in semitones. A third can be major or minor, for example, even though both span three letter names.

Notice: C–F♯ and C–G♭ are both six semitones, but one is a fourth and the other is a fifth. This is why enharmonic spelling matters. We won't cover every interval quality today; we'll return to these when we build chords.

Compare two kinds of third
C–E♭ is a minor third (3 semitones); C–E is a major third (4 semitones).
